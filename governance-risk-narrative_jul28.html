How-to Deep Dive

Governance & Risk

Governance is not one thing that happens once. As you transform a workflow, several different kinds of governance touch it at different points: how you classify and handle the data, how the AI system itself is reviewed and registered, what security controls apply, whether financial reporting is affected, and how the change is documented and communicated. This chapter walks the six steps and tells you which governance touchpoint applies where, so you do not need to become a risk officer to cover them. One principle sits above everything else, and it is true for every touchpoint: you are accountable for what AI produces on your behalf. The AI is not.

📌 Governance is not a separate job. It is a small set of habits attached to steps you are already doing, and one rule decides how much applies to you: the moment a use case goes beyond personal productivity, it must be registered.

Three governance gaps show up often enough to name: work that is registered nowhere, so it is invisible to the enterprise, uncounted, unsupported, and a control gap waiting to be found by an audit instead of a colleague; low-code and agent tools that proliferate without review, where an assistant someone built for themselves quietly becomes something a whole team relies on, with no registration, no testing, and no owner; and no clear place to get domain-specific risk answers, so teams either guess or stall. This chapter addresses all three, and the fix is smaller than most teams fear.

What can actually go wrong. The risks below are the ones that show up in practice. You do not need to memorize them; you need to recognize them when you see them, because the controls in this chapter map to them directly.

RiskWhat it looks like in practice
Data qualityPoor input data, biased, incomplete, inaccurate, or missing context, produces poor AI results. The output is only as good as what went in.
BiasAI can perpetuate or introduce bias from its training data, cultural context, or technical limitations, showing up as misclassifications, skewed results, or assumptions applied unevenly.
HallucinationAI generates inaccurate or untruthful information that looks reasonable and true. If you did not verify it, you shipped it.
DriftAs real-world data evolves, model performance inherently degrades over time, producing less accurate or less reliable results if nobody is watching.
Transparency and explainabilityWithout visibility into a model's design, training data, and decision logic, an AI system can function as a black box, limiting auditability and validation of its outputs.
Incomplete or inaccurate resultsAI may omit relevant data when generating a response, or produce a result that is plausible but factually wrong, nonsensical, or describes something that never happened.
Prompt injectionMalicious or misleading instructions embedded in content the AI reads can manipulate its behavior, resulting in data leakage, bypassed safeguards, or harmful outputs.
Data privacy and confidentialityAI systems may ingest sensitive or personal data without proper controls and expose confidential information through their outputs. Inputs are a risk too: a file uploaded into an agent can persist there or elsewhere, and logging interactions with the AI in an unsecured location creates the same exposure as the original data.
General unreliabilityAll of the above add up to one rule: AI output is not guaranteed to be correct. Always verify.

How much governance does your use case need? IBM's GenAI Practical Guidance defines four levels, based on who relies on the output, not on how sophisticated the AI is. Be honest about which level you are at, and move up a level (and register) the moment your use case does too.

The key rule from IBM's GenAI Practical Guidance: any AI use case beyond low-risk personal productivity must be registered in IGMS. High-risk use cases (per the Risk Review Needed list) require additional review regardless of level, including at the personal-productivity level.

Know your data, register your work.

Step 1

Data governance: know your data before it touches a model

Every governance conversation eventually comes back to the data, so start there. Most organizations, IBM included, classify data into four tiers, from freely shareable to tightly controlled. The tier determines what tools you may use, what approvals you need, and how much review the use case attracts.

TierWhat it meansExamples
PublicFreely shareable, no restrictions.Content already published on ibm.com, press releases, public marketing material.
InternalFor IBM only. Use approved, SSO-authenticated tools.Internal policies, non-public IBM data, general project plans.
ConfidentialNeeds extra care regardless of tool approval, due to legal, contractual, or reputational risk.HR data, M&A data, audit data, fraud and compliance data, cybersecurity and threat data, sensitive financial data.
RestrictedMust never go into an AI tool, or only under specific controls named in the tool's Terms of Use.Protected Health Information, export-controlled data, Controlled Unclassified Information, proprietary source code, client data under NDA.

One rule cuts across all four tiers: client data is not IBM data. If a use case needs client data, check the contract with Legal for what it permits before using AI on it at all.

Approved tools are easy to spot: they use SSO with your IBM w3 ID, and Copilot Chat shows a green shield when it is in an approved configuration. When unsure, check the Approved AI Tools Matrix rather than guessing.

In depth: Global Privacy Assessments and data quality

If your AI system is deployed internally and processes personal data, IBM may require a Global Privacy Assessment (GPA) alongside your IGMS registration; your Integrated Governance Lead can tell you if it applies. Data quality is part of governance too, not just engineering. A model trained or grounded on stale, incomplete, or wrong data produces confident, wrong answers at scale. The Chief Data Office offers data quality services you can use to check sources before you build on them, and the Tech & Data Assessment you are already running in this step (see the Systems Integration chapter) is where those checks belong.

⚠ Pain point
Confidential data in the wrong tool
One of the more preventable AI incidents is not a sophisticated attack. It is a well-meaning person pasting confidential, personal, or client material into a tool that was not approved to hold it, usually to save time. Every tool's Terms of Use spells out what is off-limits: Copilot, for example, must never receive Protected Health Information, export-controlled data, client materials under NDA, proprietary IBM source code, or Controlled Unclassified Information. The two habits that prevent an incident: know the classification of what you are handling, and check the tool's Terms of Use before you paste anything you would not want made public. If your data type is not on the approved listing, or you are unsure, the Chief Data Office can run a data risk review.
Step 2

Register the use case and screen the risk

In depth: why registration is worth the two minutes

Registration is the habit teams most easily skip, because it feels like bureaucracy at the exact moment enthusiasm is highest. Reframe it: IGMS is the enterprise inventory of AI models, systems, and use cases, and it is where required risk reviews start. A registered use case is visible, counted in what the enterprise knows about its own AI, and supported when something goes wrong. An unregistered one is invisible when you need that support most. The assessments are routed automatically to the owners you named, and if your use case builds on an AI system that was already reviewed, a new review is only needed when the new use triggers heightened risk.

Screen the tier honestly with your IGL, check whether anything about the use case appears on the risk-review list, and put the answer in front of leadership as part of the go/no-go. Heightened risk does not mean no. It means the review will involve the PAID-COE and Privacy + AI Legal, with cost and timeline implications worth scoping before the build starts, so they never become a week-eight surprise.

All three paths register you in IGMS automatically. A handful of other systems (Asset Management, Clearing House, the AI Model & Data Catalog) also feed IGMS directly, so registration may already be underway without you initiating it.
⚠ Pain point
The unregistered pilot that became infrastructure
A pilot gets built to prove a point. It works, the team starts relying on it, and six months later it is load-bearing infrastructure that exists in no inventory, passed no review, and has no named owner. This is a common path to shadow AI, and low-code tools have made it easier to walk. The fix costs minutes at the start: the moment others rely on the output, register it. Retroactive registration after an audit finding costs far more, in every sense.
✓

Ready to move on when

The data is classified with steward approval where needed, every tool in the plan is approved, the use case is registered in IGMS (or confirmed personal-productivity tier), owners are named, and the go/no-go included the risk screen.

Discover Governance & Risk Steps 3 & 4 · Workflow Analysis · Solution Design

Design the controls in: oversight, logging, requirements.

📌 Step 3 decides where humans stay in the loop and what gets logged. Step 4 turns those decisions into build requirements. Controls designed here cost a fraction of controls retrofitted after the build, and they are the difference between oversight that works and oversight that exists on paper.
Who to involve
Domain transformation lead Project architect Product owner Integrated Governance Lead Security contact (if needed) Topic SMEs (as needed)
What to design in Discover for Governance & Risk
1
Design human oversight into the future-state workflow. Decide where people review, approve, and override, and make those moments real work steps with time budgeted, not checkboxes. Heightened-risk systems will likely require direct human approval on outputs.
2
Decide what gets logged. Inputs, outputs, overrides, and decisions, enough that six months from now you can answer "why did it do that?" for any output that mattered.
3
Pull in the right SMEs for the knowledge your use case touches. Legal content goes to your business unit legal contact, HR topics to AskHR, accounting treatment to your accounting focal.
4
Write the controls as requirements. Accuracy thresholds, safety and PII guardrails, and a user-facing notice wherever people interact directly with the AI (IBM's Carbon for AI icon is the standard way to show it). Be transparent about where AI is used in the process with colleagues too, not only end users. If it is not in the requirements, it will not be in the build.
5
Document the model choices. A Fact Sheet documenting why this model, the performance metrics you will capture, and a plain-language description of what the system is for and what its limits are (for third-party models, link the vendor's Model Card instead).
6
If financial reporting is touched, design the controls with Finance. An AI solution that affects financial reporting needs controls addressing misstatement risk (the risk that a financial report contains a material error), and one that executes a key control without human intervention requires a designed, operating control around it.
Step 3

Human oversight and logging are workflow design, not paperwork

As you analyze the workflow in Step 3, decide specifically: which outputs does a human review before they take effect, who has the authority to override, and what happens to the work when they do? Give those people the time and the information to do it, not just the responsibility.

In depth: what "meaningful" oversight actually requires

AI systems at IBM are deployed with the expectation that humans oversee them meaningfully throughout their lifecycle, and "meaningfully" is the word that does the work. An approval step that a person has no time, context, or authority to actually perform is not oversight; it only looks like oversight, which hides the gap instead of closing it. The Adoption & Change chapter's training guidance (what to do when the AI is wrong) is the other half of this design.

Logging follows from the same question. Log what you would need to reconstruct a decision: what went in, what came out, who reviewed it, what they changed. For most workflow solutions this is a modest requirement that is nearly free to build in and nearly impossible to retrofit.

⚠ Pain point
Oversight on paper
The design says "human reviews all outputs." The human has 40 outputs an hour and 8 seconds each. Every review passes, because passing is the only thing there is time for. When something goes wrong, the review log shows a person approved it, but the design set them up to miss it, not the reviewer. If the volume makes genuine review impossible, say so in Discover and design differently: sample-based review, tiered confidence routing, or a narrower scope. Honest oversight design is a favor to the people who will do the overseeing.
Step 4

Controls become requirements

Everything decided in Step 3 goes into the build-ready requirements alongside the functional ones: the guardrails (what the system must refuse or filter), the thresholds (what accuracy is acceptable for this use, and what happens below it), the transparency notice, and the logging. Add the Fact Sheet from the checklist above. None of it is long. All of it is much easier to write now than to reconstruct later.

In depth: two more design-time rules

First, internal builds follow the client zero standards (IBM uses its own products internally first, before selling them): IBM products and services, on strategic platforms, with IBM models first, and a documented exception process where a genuine gap exists (see the build, buy, or hybrid guidance in Systems Integration, Step 4). Second, where third-party AI components are in the solution anyway, request their model card or responsible-AI documentation and link it in your Fact Sheet, rather than treating the component as a black box.

If you are building with low-code or agent tools: the same rules apply, and it is easy to miss them here. An agent built in an afternoon is still an AI system. The moment a team relies on it, it needs registration, an owner, and the oversight design above. Keep human review as the default for anything an agent does that others consume, and be deliberate about what data and tools the agent can reach, because an agent's risk surface is defined by its permissions, not its prompt. If the build uses Bob, the same governance applies plus a few tool-specific habits: never use auto-enable mode, disable telemetry collection, keep Sensitive Personal Information and similarly restricted data out of it entirely, and register anything beyond personal productivity through AskIntegrity just like any other AI system.

⚠ Pain point
A point-in-time review is not continuous assurance
Security and control reviews assess your use case at a moment in time, against its design. Passing them does not mean the solution is secure forever, and going through the governance process does not transfer your accountability to the reviewers. Treat reviews as a floor, not a ceiling. When the solution changes, the reviews' conclusions age accordingly, which is why revalidation (Step 6) exists.
✓

Ready to move on when

The oversight model names who reviews what and gives them time to do it, the logging plan can reconstruct any decision that matters, controls and thresholds are written into the build-ready requirements, a Fact Sheet documents the model choices, and any required SME and security conversations have happened.

Execute Governance & Risk Steps 5 & 6 · Experimentation · Scale & Adopt

Prove the controls, then keep them alive.

📌 Test to the thresholds you set, attack your own system before someone else does, and treat launch as the start of governance, not the end: monitoring beyond uptime, incident paths wired, and the record revalidated whenever anything changes.
Who to involve
Developer lead Project architect Product owner Security contact (if engaged) Business and technical owners (post-launch)
What to do in Execute for Governance & Risk
1
Test to the thresholds from Solution Design: accuracy against the bar you set, plus safety, hate/abuse/profanity, and PII handling at the system level. Fairness and bias testing applies where the risk review requires it.
2
Red-team the solution. Adversarial prompts, out-of-scope requests, prompt injection through any content the system reads, and permission boundaries for anything agentic. ARES (the AI Robustness Evaluation System) is IBM's automated tool for this.
3
Validate that the guardrails block what testing found. A vulnerability discovered in testing and not turned into a runtime control is effort spent that never pays off. Close the loop: turn every finding into a control. UAT (User Acceptance Testing) must include what users do when the AI is wrong.
4
Stand up monitoring beyond uptime: output quality trends, drift against the baseline, and override rates. A system can be 100% available and quietly wrong.
5
Wire the incident paths before launch. A feedback route users will actually use, vulnerability reporting through PSIRT (Product Security Incident Response Team), and incident reporting through CSIRT (Computer Security Incident Response Team). Everyone on the team should know which path is for what.
6
Revalidate the IGMS record at least annually, and any time the AI changes: a model swap, new AI functionality, a process change, or agents being turned on. Keep the system description and value details current.
Step 5

Experimentation: prove the controls, not just the features

The sprint validates that the solution works. This chapter's addition: the sprint also validates that the controls work, and the only way to know is to attack them. Feed the system inputs it should refuse. Put instructions inside documents it reads and see whether it follows them. Ask it to act outside its scope and check what the permission boundaries actually permit.

In depth: why this is the good place to find problems

Prompt injection through content the AI reads is a known, demonstrated failure mode for unhardened systems, so assume yours is vulnerable until testing shows otherwise. Every failure found here is cheap to fix. The same failure found in production, by a user or an auditor, costs a lot more, so this is the good place to find it.

Treat overrides and refusals during UAT as governance data, the same way the Adoption & Change chapter treats them as adoption data. If reviewers are overriding a third of outputs, either the threshold is wrong, the model is wrong, or the oversight design is carrying more load than it was built for. All three are worth catching in the sprint, while they are still cheap to fix.

⚠ Pain point
Testing only the happy path
The demo works because the demo was designed to work: clean inputs, expected questions, cooperative users. Production brings malformed inputs, adversarial content, and people probing what the system will do. If your test plan contains nothing designed to make the system fail, you have not tested it; you have rehearsed it. Budget real sprint time for adversarial testing, and re-run it when the model or the tools change, because behavior changes with them.
Step 6

Scale & Adopt: governance is continuous, not a launch gate

The habits in the checklist above only work if someone is actually watching. A dashboard nobody looks at is not monitoring, and an incident path nobody remembers is not a plan. Pick a real cadence for the monitoring review, name who owns it, and treat the first bad day as the test of whether the incident paths actually work, not the moment to invent them.

The revalidation loop is what keeps every approval the system carries describing a system that still exists.

Ownership carries this. The Business Lead, Technical Lead, and Global Process Owner named in Engage own the running system, and the transition to them (covered in Systems Integration's handoff guidance) includes the monitoring dashboards, the incident runbook, and the revalidation calendar. Governance that lives with named owners survives team changes. Governance that lived with the project team leaves with them.

⚠ Pain point
The silent change
Six months in, someone swaps the model version, adds a tool to the agent, or points the system at a new data source. Behavior changes; nobody re-tests; the IGMS record still describes the system as it launched. Every review and approval the system carries now describes something that no longer exists. The rule that prevents it: any AI change re-triggers the Step 5 tests that apply and a revalidation of the record. Put it in the change process, not in people's memories.
✓

Governance is holding when

Thresholds passed with adversarial testing done, guardrails proven against what testing found, monitoring is watched by a named owner, the team knows the incident paths, and the IGMS record matches the system that is actually running.
