Key Challenge

Prioritization & Roadmap

Most teams don't fail because they chose the wrong technology. They fail because they chose the wrong problem, tried to do too many things at once, or never made a clear decision to stop when the evidence said to. This chapter walks through how to pick the right workflows, validate them quickly, and build a delivery plan that compounds over time.

Engage Tech & Data Assessment · Business Process Mapping

Use what you already know. Pick what you can actually win.

By the time you're ready to prioritize, Engage has given you two assets: a picture of your tech and data readiness — which tells you where AI can realistically be built — and a map of your business processes — which tells you where fixing something is actually worth money. Good prioritization matches those two pictures. Without both, you're either picking the exciting idea or the feasible one. Rarely the right one.

The goal is a short, scored list of real candidates. For each one, you're answering four questions: How much is this worth if we fix it? Can we build it with what we have today? Does a senior leader care enough to commit to it? And is there a real path to shipping something within 90 days?

Score every candidate on value potential, data readiness, technical feasibility, and strategic fit. The scoring is the deliverable — it's what makes your recommendation defensible when a leader pushes back.
⚠ Pain point
Picking based on opinions, not evidence
The most common failure here is running a workshop, discussing what "feels" most impactful, and producing a ranking that reflects political momentum more than real opportunity. When that list goes to leadership, it gets overridden — and the team has no data to push back with. The fix requires discipline: use a consistent scoring rubric for every candidate, show the math, and let the numbers drive the conversation. Your recommendation is only as strong as the evidence behind it.

Also look outside your organization: what are comparable companies doing with similar processes? Benchmarks tell you what's actually achievable — so you're aiming at a real target rather than an improvement to a broken baseline — and they give leadership a concrete frame for why this investment makes sense.

⚠ Pain point
Choosing the easy fix instead of the real problem
When time is short, teams gravitate toward workflows that are easy to improve rather than ones that matter. The result is a technically successful build that nobody cares about scaling. Look for root causes when you score candidates — if a workflow's real problem is bad data or a process that shouldn't exist, those issues should show up in the feasibility score, not get papered over.

Before finalizing the list, check one more thing: confirm that a senior leader will actually commit to each top candidate. Not verbally support it — actually show up to weekly steering meetings, clear blockers, and make hard scope calls when needed. If no one at that level is willing to do that, the project will stall the moment it hits its first obstacle. Drop those candidates now.

1
Score all candidates using the prioritization template — value, feasibility, data readiness, strategic fit. Show the math.
2
Pull external benchmarks for each top candidate. What does a leading organization's version of this process look like?
3
Confirm a sponsor for the top candidates before finalizing. No sponsor = drop it.
4
Sanity-check timelines against developer capacity. Scope that can't ship in 90 days needs to be flagged now.

Discover Workflow Analysis · Solution Design

Prove the opportunity is real. Then define exactly what you're building.

Discover has two jobs. First, you validate that the workflow you chose is actually worth transforming — not because it scored well on paper, but because the evidence holds up when you look closely. Second, if it does, you define precisely what you're going to build. Both are distinct. Don't skip the first because you're confident; don't rush the second because you're excited.

Step 3

Workflow Analysis — Validate the opportunity

The Workflow Analysis is a time-boxed investigation — typically two weeks — into the workflow you selected. You're gathering: how much it costs to run today, what skills it requires, what the key performance indicators are, what a good version of it looks like at a peer organization, and most importantly, what's actually causing it to perform poorly right now. Your output is a recommendation: go deeper into design, or move on to the next workflow.

The most useful interviews are with the people who do the work, not the managers who oversee it. Documented processes and actual practice diverge constantly. Frontline users know where the friction is, what workarounds exist, and what would genuinely make their job easier — and that knowledge is what makes your analysis real rather than theoretical.

⚠ Pain point
The go/no-go decision that never gets made
The hardest moment in this phase isn't the analysis — it's having an honest conversation about whether to proceed. Teams naturally want to move forward. Sponsors have staked credibility on their workflow selection. The mechanism to protect against this is simple: book the go/no-go meeting with the steering committee before the analysis starts, and brief the sponsor that they may hear a recommendation to stop. A no-go isn't a failure — it means the investigation worked. Without a hard date and explicit setup, the meeting drifts, the decision softens, and a marginal workflow goes into design and becomes a failed build months later.
The Workflow Analysis follows four investigative tracks — spend, user research, benchmarks, and root cause — then converges on a single go/no-go question. Both outcomes are valid. Document either one and move accordingly.

Case StudyState University — How the go/no-go worked

Before starting the analysis, the project lead booked a go/no-go meeting with the VP of Enrollment and technical architect for week four. The analysis found: 14-day average document review time, 60% of counselor hours spent on manual verification, no standardized checklist, and peer universities achieving same-day verification using AI document processing. The team arrived at the meeting with a clear recommendation to proceed — and the VP approved staffing within 48 hours.

Step 4

Solution Design — Define exactly what you're building

With a confirmed opportunity and approval to proceed, Solution Design answers two questions: what does the ideal end state look like, and what's the smallest version of it that we can ship in 90 days to test whether the approach is right?

The ideal end state — the "future state vision" — should be described without reference to how things work today. Don't design around your current systems or inherited constraints. Describe what would happen if you were designing this workflow from scratch with today's technology. That picture becomes the reference point for every scope decision you make after this.

⚠ Pain point
Handing over requirements no one can build from
This is the most common handoff failure in the entire process. Business teams write requirements that make sense internally but are too conceptual for a development team to act on. The test is simple: give a developer the requirements document and ask if they can write a user story from it without asking you a single question. If they can't, it's not ready. This isn't about making the document longer — it's about being specific: who does what, in what system, with what data, and what does done look like.

MVP scope — what ships in 90 days — should be decided by the team together and signed off by the executive sponsor. Just as important as the in-scope list is the explicit out-of-scope list. Every feature that isn't named as out-of-scope is implicitly in scope, and that's what causes delivery delays. Write both lists. Get them approved. The out-of-scope list automatically becomes the post-MVP backlog.

All five workstreams run in parallel from Day 1 — not sequentially. Change management and value tracking start before any code is written. The go/no-go decision at Day 90 is made against the KPI baseline set before the sprint began.
1
Book the go/no-go meeting before the analysis starts. Date on the calendar, sponsor briefed that either answer is acceptable.
2

3

4

5

6

Step 5

Experimentation — 90-day build and go/no-go

The build team's first task — before any code — is to read the requirements and confirm the scope is achievable in 90 days. If it isn't, that conversation needs to happen immediately. A scope problem caught on day two costs nothing. The same problem discovered in week six of a sprint costs the delivery date and executive confidence.

Run the build in two-week sprint cycles. What you test first matters: put the riskiest assumption — the thing most likely to invalidate the whole approach — in Sprint 1, while there's still room to pivot. The steering committee's job is to clear blockers and surface problems, not to receive good news. As Sandy put it from the Source-to-Pay transformation: "Red on a chart is good." A build that shows only green is either measuring the wrong things or reporting selectively.

⚠ Pain point
Success metrics set after the build
If you haven't locked the go/no-go criteria before Day 1, they'll be defined after the build — which means they'll be defined to pass. The Day 90 decision needs to answer a pre-agreed question: did this build demonstrate enough value, at enough reliability, to justify scaling? Write that question down. Agree on the data source. Get sponsor sign-off before development starts. A metric with no pre-agreed threshold is decoration, not a decision rule.

Scope reductions during the build are not failures — they're the mechanism that makes 90-day delivery work. The MCC Marketing transformation de-scoped an agent mid-build when it became too large for the timeline, and leadership recognized it as the right call. What determines whether a de-scope preserves or destroys confidence is how it's communicated: show what was cut, why, when it comes back, and what the team is protecting by cutting it.

Step 6

Scale & Adopt — After the decision

The go/no-go decision at Day 90 is not the end of the roadmap — it's the start of the next one. If it's a go, the post-MVP backlog activates and change management goes into full execution: training, communications, retiring the old process, and monitoring whether people are actually using the new one. If it's a no-go, the documentation of what you learned is the input to the next workflow analysis.

Before the transformation team steps back, three ownership questions need explicit answers: who manages the business content and processes around the tool? Who maintains the technical infrastructure and keeps the system running? Who handles user questions and day-to-day support? When those three roles are unnamed, the solution degrades quietly and eventually gets abandoned.

1
Re-validate scope with the build team before Day 1. If anything isn't achievable, surface it immediately.
2
Lock go/no-go metrics and data source before any code is written. The Day 90 decision needs a pre-agreed question and answer standard.
3
Test the riskiest assumption first. Whatever would most likely invalidate the whole approach goes into Sprint 1.
4
Communicate scope reductions clearly — what moved, why, and when it returns. Activate the post-MVP backlog with owners.
5
Name three owners before handoff: business content, technical infrastructure, user support. Unnamed ownership means the solution drifts.
6
Return to the prioritized list with what you learned. The next workflow analysis should be faster than the first.
