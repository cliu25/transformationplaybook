Systems Integration

Most AI projects don't fail because the model isn't good enough. They fail because the data pipeline breaks, the integration is too brittle to scale, or no one knows how to maintain it after the build team leaves. This chapter covers how to surface integration complexity early, write requirements that developers can build from, navigate the path to production at IBM, and hand off a solution that survives after the sprint ends.

Engage Systems Integration Step 1 & 2 · Tech & Data Assessment

Know what you're working with before you commit.

📌 Before you design anything, confirm what systems exist, whether the data in them is actually usable, who owns access, and whether a pre-built connector already solves part of the problem.
Who to involve
Domain focal CIO/BPT architect Data or platform owner Process SMEs
How to assess integration readiness
1
Work with the domain focal to inventory all platforms in use: CRM, ERP, content repositories, collaboration tools, and any custom or proprietary systems.
2
Bring in the CIO/BPT architect to validate each system: Does an API exist? Is it documented? What are the access requirements? Who owns it? What does the platform roadmap look like?
3
Check data quality and freshness on a real sample from each source. Nulls, stale data, and format inconsistencies are constraints to design around, not surprises to discover mid-build.
4
Identify named resources from each platform team who will support the transformation. Platform ownership outside your domain is the most common cause of stagnation.
5
Ask the architect whether ARB or security review applies to your build. It is not required for every AI project, but where it does apply, start the process immediately. Flag any major structural blockers to domain VP and CIO leadership before finalizing the design.
IBM runs on modern enterprise platforms. The integration risk is rarely "this system is too old." It is usually access controls, data freshness, and platform team capacity. Document the constraints early so they shape the design, not stop the build.
⚠ Pain point
Assuming data is ready because it exists
Data that exists in a platform is not the same as data that is clean, accessible, and in a format your pipeline can consume. A field that updates weekly instead of in real time changes what your AI can do. Run a quality check on a real sample now. If 30% of key fields are incomplete, that is a constraint to plan around, not a surprise for Sprint 3.
🔴 When to escalate
Small issues (missing API key, undocumented endpoint, format inconsistency): work directly with the platform team. Large structural blockers (platform migration, multi-month security review, major infrastructure investment): escalate to the domain VP and CIO leadership before the design is finalized. Cross-org communication stalled: escalate to the AIFT team (Radha). Enterprise coordination is part of what AIFT exists to do.
Why this matters: full context and common patterns

The Technology and Data Assessment is where you surface the constraints that will shape every decision downstream. The goal is not to fix anything. It is to document what is real, using the same five questions for every system you touch:

DimensionQuestion to pressure testSignal it's ready
ArchitectureDoes it fit into our systems and data, and is there a documented API?Standard, documented integration point
Access & ownershipWho owns it, and what is the approval process to connect?Named owner, known request path
Data freshnessIs the data real-time, or does it update on a delay?Update cadence documented and workable for your use case
Data qualityIs the data clean, complete, and usable, not just present?Sample checked; nulls and format issues known
GovernanceDoes this touch critical infrastructure or need ARB / security review?Reviewed with the CIO/BPT architect; timeline known

IBM operates on modern enterprise platforms, so the answer to "does it exist" is almost always yes. The real risk sits in the other four rows: freshness, ownership, quality, and governance. Surfacing those in Engage, not mid-build, is the entire point of this step.

Check what already exists before you scope custom work. watsonx Orchestrate has over 150 pre-built connectors to platforms like Salesforce, SAP, ServiceNow, and Slack. Before you assume an integration point needs to be built from scratch, check the Orchestrate connector catalog with the CIO/BPT architect. This check belongs here, in Engage, because it affects whether an integration is even worth prioritizing, not just how you build it once it is in scope. The full build-vs-buy decision for each connection point happens in Solution Design (Step 4).

Where ARB does apply, it has taken four to six weeks on past IBM projects. If that dependency surfaces in week eight of a twelve-week build, you have already missed your date. Ask the architect early, and start the process well before Discovery ends if it is required.

One more pattern worth knowing: the platforms your solution depends on are often owned by teams outside the domain, not by anyone on your project. The fix is not to work around them. It is to identify a named contact on each platform team early and bring them into the conversation before you are blocked on them.

Case StudyState University: discovering the integration landscape

In week one of Engage, the enrollment team worked with the university's IT architect to map every system touched by the document verification workflow. Box was the document repository, the admissions CRM held applicant records, and a third system managed enrollment decisions. Box had a well-documented API and the team already had credentials. The CRM required a separate access request through the IT governance process. The enrollment decision system had no API at all, only a weekly batch export to a shared folder.

The team documented all three. They started the CRM access request immediately, which took two weeks to clear. The batch export on the enrollment system was flagged as a constraint: any AI that depended on real-time enrollment status would not work. That single finding changed the scope of the solution before a line of requirements was written.

Step 3

Trace how data moves through the current process, end to end. The example below shows what this looked like for a university document verification workflow, including the constraints that came out of the assessment and how they shaped the MVP.

⚠ Pain point
Discovering integration blockers during the build
Integration complexity that surfaces mid-build stops progress while the team scrambles to map systems they should have documented weeks earlier. The two weeks spent on Workflow Analysis prevents two months of mid-sprint delays.
Why this matters: what "trace the data flow" means

For the workflow you are transforming, trace how data actually moves: where it originates, what systems it passes through, what transformations happen along the way, and where it ends up. The process SMEs tell you how the workflow runs. The CIO/BPT architect tells you how the data moves underneath it. Both views are required.

Document every integration point, every manual handoff, and every place where data quality degrades or updates are delayed. Also identify the non-functional requirements that will constrain your design: whether ARB review applies (this depends on what you are connecting to and how the solution is registered, not a blanket rule), security review requirements, data residency rules, and compliance constraints. Where ARB does apply, it is a hard gate. It has taken four to six weeks on past IBM builds, and if you discover that requirement in week eight of a twelve-week build, you have missed your delivery date.

Step 4

Solution Design: integration decisions and build-ready requirements

For each connection point, decide whether to use a pre-built connector or build custom, then write requirements specific enough that a developer never has to stop and ask a clarifying question.

How to write build-ready requirements
1
Map the current-state architecture for the selected workflow with the process SMEs and CIO/BPT architect. Document every system, integration point, and data flow.
2
Ask the architect whether ARB or security review applies to this integration. Not every AI build needs it; it depends on the systems involved and how the solution is registered. Where it does apply, start the process immediately. It has taken four to six weeks on past IBM builds.
3
Check the watsonx Orchestrate connector catalog for each integration point. Use a pre-built connector where it covers 80%+ of the requirement. Build where it does not.
4
Write build-ready requirements covering persona, data objects, source systems, API details, authentication, error handling, acceptance criteria, and fallback paths.
5
Test requirements with the architect and product owner. If a developer would need to ask clarifying questions, the requirements are not ready. Add specificity until they are.
6
Document the integration approach for each connection point with reasoning. You will need to defend it when questions arise during the build.
Handoff Tracking belongs in these requirements too. The metrics defined in Value Measurement specify what the solution must capture from Sprint 1.
For each connection point: build, buy, or hybrid?
Build: use IBM's stack
Build with watsonx and the Orchestrate connector catalog. IBM acquisitions count as our stack.
Buy: go external
IBM does not have the right fit for this layer yet. Frontier models are the clearest example.
Hybrid: combine
IBM capabilities plus external tools for the gaps. This is also how ecosystem partnerships start.
The trap: defaulting to buy for speed without checking lock-in, data exposure, and total cost. Buying does not mean it plugs in seamlessly. Integration work still applies.
⚠ Pain point
Requirements too conceptual to build from
"Integrate with the CRM to pull applicant data" sounds complete but leaves a developer with twenty questions: which object, which fields, what authentication, what happens on error? If the product owner and architect cannot confirm a developer can start without clarification, the requirements are not ready.
Why this matters: build vs. buy reasoning and what "build-ready" means

With the technical baseline documented, Solution Design answers two questions: what integration approach will you use for each connection point, and what exactly needs to be built? Both need to be resolved before the build team starts. A vague answer to either question means the build team will stop and ask, and that is when your timeline slips.

Build vs. buy considerations. You checked the Orchestrate connector catalog in Engage to see what already exists. Now, for each specific connection point in your build-ready requirements, make the call: when a pre-built connector covers 80% of what you need, configure and extend it. When the requirements are specific enough that a pre-built connector would require more customization work than building from scratch, build. Document the reasoning either way. You will need to defend the decision later.

Write build-ready requirements. The test: give the CIO/BPT architect and the product owner the requirements document and ask whether a developer can write a user story from it without a clarification call. Build-ready requirements include the persona, the specific data objects (fields, format, source system), the systems involved (API endpoint, authentication method, rate limits), the acceptance criteria, and the fallback path when the integration fails. If any of those are missing or vague, the developer will stop and ask, and that is when your timeline slips.

Case StudyState University: integration decisions in Solution Design

The Workflow Analysis confirmed three integration points: the admissions CRM (applicant records), Box (document storage), and the enrollment decision system (batch export only). For the CRM, the CIO/BPT architect confirmed a documented Salesforce API was available and the access request was already in progress. For Box, a pre-built connector in the watsonx environment covered the file access pattern they needed. For the enrollment system, the batch-only limitation ruled it out for the MVP. It was explicitly moved to Wave 2.

Requirements were reviewed by the product owner and the architect together. The original requirement read "check Box for required documents." The final version specified the exact document types (transcript, government ID, personal statement), the Box folder structure, the API call pattern, the field that would be written back to the CRM on completion, and the exception path when a document failed validation. The developer started Sprint 1 without a single clarification call.

Execute Systems Integration Step 5 & 6 · Experimentation · Scale & Adopt

Build, integrate, instrument, and hand off cleanly.

📌 Connect to real systems from Sprint 1, test every way the integration can fail, instrument it so problems surface before users report them, and hand off to one named person before the build team steps back.
Who to involve
CIO/BPT architect Developer lead Product owner Platform team (for production) Named technical owner
How to build and hand off cleanly
1
Connect to real systems in Sprint 1. Validate real data flows through the pipeline before UAT begins. Do not use mocked data to test integration.
2
Build error handling and logging from day one. Every API call needs a failure path. Every failure needs a log entry and an alert.
3
Test every failure scenario explicitly: API timeout, malformed data, authentication failure, rate limit hit, source system unavailable.
4
Confirm with the architect whether ARB review applies to your build, and if so, keep that process moving alongside the build. The CIO/BPT architect is the bridge to the platform team and, where relevant, the ARB reviewer.
5
Name a specific technical owner before the build team steps back. Not a team: a person. Walk them through the code, run through failure scenarios together.
6
Complete the technical handoff checklist before stepping back. Code access, credentials, architecture diagram, runbook, monitoring, owner name, and escalation path.
Step 5

Experimentation: build and validate on real data

Build error handling from the first day and instrument everything before it goes to production. You need to know when the pipeline fails before users report it.

Test both the happy path and every failure mode. If you have not tested a failure scenario, it will happen in production. UAT should only begin after end-to-end integration is validated on real data, not mocked data.
⚠ Pain point
Navigating delivery pressure vs. building something robust
Under time pressure, teams skip error handling, test with mocked data, or defer monitoring to "after launch." Each shortcut creates a production system that fails quietly and takes weeks to diagnose. The platform team will not accept a solution without error handling and monitoring, so building them in Sprint 1 is faster than retrofitting them later.
Why this matters: error handling, instrumentation, and testing

Connect to the real systems in Sprint 1 and run real data through the pipeline. The quality issues you find here are confirmations of what you documented in Engage, not surprises. The difference is that now you handle them in code. Build error handling from the first day: every API call can fail, every data source can return unexpected formats, and every system can go down. Your code needs to handle all of those gracefully, log the error, alert the right person, and either retry or fail safely.

Instrument everything. Set up logging and monitoring before anything goes to production. You need to know when the pipeline fails before users report it. Track API call success rates, data validation pass rates, processing times, and error frequencies. These are not just operational metrics. They are the evidence base for whether the solution is working in practice and whether it is ready to scale.

Test failure scenarios as deliberately as you test the happy path. API timeout, malformed data, authentication failure, rate limit hit, source system unavailable. If you have not tested a failure mode, it will happen in production.

Navigating path to production at IBM
📌 Your CIO/BPT architect is a partner in building; the platform team decides whether you can deploy. These are two different relationships with two different timelines. ARB review is conditional, not universal. Ask the architect early whether it applies to your specific build.
Why this matters: how the CIO organization fits together

One of the most common points of confusion in IBM AI transformations is the path to production. The CIO/BPT organization plays two roles: they provide development resources through their architect and build capacity, and they own the platform teams that control the production environment. Your architect is a partner in building. The platform team controls whether you can deploy. These are separate conversations with separate timelines.

The key to moving through the path to production is to enable your architects to solve the right problems and the big problems rather than being pulled into delivery work that keeps them from seeing the broader picture. When architects are focused on the right problems, they can tell you early whether ARB review applies to your build, help you navigate platform requirements, anticipate feedback before any formal review, and connect you to the right platform team contacts. When they are heads-down in day-to-day delivery, those strategic connections do not happen.

Practically: ask the architect in Step 3 (Workflow Analysis) whether your build requires ARB review. It typically applies to registered CIO agents and integrations touching critical infrastructure, not to every AI project. If it does apply, start that process immediately rather than after requirements are complete. Bring the architect to the initial steering committee meeting so the CIO organization understands the solution from the start. When you hit a platform requirement that looks like a blocker, the architect is the person who knows whether it is a real blocker or a standard process with a known path through it.

Step 6

Scale & Adopt: hand off cleanly and maintain what was built

A clean technical handoff means one specific person, not a team, takes ownership of the integration. Before the build team steps back, complete a technical handoff checklist and confirm the named owner can actually maintain what they are taking over.

Handoff The technical handoff is half the job. Retiring the old process and monitoring adoption after launch is covered in Adoption & Change, Execute.
The named technical owner is not just a name on paper. They need to have been trained on the solution, run through failure scenarios with the build team, and confirmed they can troubleshoot independently. If the owner cannot answer basic questions about how the integration works, the handoff is not complete.
⚠ Pain point
No named technical owner after handoff
Handing off to "the IT team" means no one owns it. When something breaks, the issue bounces between teams and the solution stops working. Name a specific person, confirm they have the skills and capacity, and put their name in every piece of handoff documentation. If you cannot name a person, the handoff is not ready.

Case StudyState University: clean handoff to the IT integration team

Before the build team stepped back, they completed a full technical handoff to a named IT integration specialist. The handoff included: documented code repository with test cases, architecture diagram showing all three system connections (Salesforce CRM, Box, and the admissions decision system), API credentials with rotation procedures, data lineage documentation, and a runbook for the five most common failure scenarios. The specialist shadowed the build team for two weeks, ran through each failure scenario, and confirmed she could troubleshoot independently.

Six months later, when the Salesforce API version was deprecated and broke the integration, the specialist diagnosed and fixed it in under two hours using the runbook, without contacting the original build team. That outcome is only possible when the handoff is complete before the build team leaves.
