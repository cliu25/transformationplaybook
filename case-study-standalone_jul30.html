Case Study

A University Transforms Student Enrollment

How a mid-sized public university applies the AIFT method, six steps, three phases, one repeatable approach, to an enrollment and document-verification transformation. Every step below is the real methodology: what to do, who's involved, what to watch for. State University's actual path through it is layered on top at each step, so you can see the method and a real example of it working together, not as two separate things.

Overview

This worked example follows State University, a university drowning in manual admissions paperwork and inconsistent outreach, from choosing an opportunity through a scaled, sustained solution. It is illustrative, a composite built from patterns across real AIFT transformations, not a literal transcript of one project.

Starting Context

State University enrolls roughly 180,000 students. The enrollment team is overwhelmed: applications, transcripts, and required documents are processed by hand, creating bottlenecks that delay onboarding and frustrate prospective students. Outreach to prospects is scattershot, staff cannot tell which applicants are most likely to enroll. University leadership has issued a mandate to modernize enrollment, but no specific AI use case has been chosen yet.

Starting point: The executive sponsor (VP of Enrollment) had a provost mandate to modernize enrollment and nothing chosen yet to build. Before anyone touched a workflow, two things had to be real: a sponsor who would actually show up, and an honest read on what the domain could build.
1. Secure a real executive sponsor

The domain focal, who'd run enrollment operations for six years, didn't take the sponsor's enthusiasm at face value, and asked for the first weekly steering meeting to be blocked on the sponsor's own calendar before kickoff even happened. Not "sounds great." A recurring 45 minutes, that week.

Twenty minutes into the first real conversation, before a single meeting had happened, the sponsor had already done the thing that separates a sponsor from an enthusiast.
⚠ Pain point
Confusing enthusiasm with commitment
Endorsement says "I think this is a good idea." Commitment says "I will act when action is needed." the domain focal had watched a different domain's project stall two months earlier for exactly this reason: a sponsor who agreed enthusiastically, then went quiet for three weeks when a data-access request needed a signature. The calendar test exists specifically so it wouldn't happen twice.
2. Name the domain focal & set the mandate

With the sponsor committed, the two of them set the mandate in one sitting: the opportunity area was enrollment and document processing, success in 90 days meant a working propensity model and faster document turnaround, and the domain focal was the domain focal, the one person running day-to-day work, not a committee. A 30-minute kickoff two days later, sponsor and domain focal only, locked the steering cadence.

3. Inventory people, processes & technology

The domain focal then ran the domain's readiness check — not a full audit, just enough to know what to avoid building around first. This covered four areas: who would be affected by any AI solution (admissions counselors, counselor managers, IT, and prospective students), what the current workflows looked like at a high level (document-heavy, manual, inconsistent), what the technology landscape was (Banner SIS, Box, Salesforce CRM, BigQuery), and what operating-model constraints existed (ARB approval required before production deployment).

4. Engage data stewards early

Before the readiness check concluded, the domain focal identified the data steward for each candidate data source — the named person accountable for quality, access, and governance — and confirmed the business case for using the data before requesting access. Data access requests without a justified business case are what cause multi-week delays mid-project.

A directional check, not a full audit. The one real flag, incomplete CRM data, became sequencing information: avoid an outreach-dependent build as the first bet.

That single finding shaped everything the domain focal looked at in Step 2: whatever workflow came first couldn't depend on the CRM's prospect data. Document verification, whatever it turned out to be, would need to lean on Banner and Box instead, both of which were already solid.

6. Set the steering cadence

The sponsor, the domain focal, and later the technical architect and the product owner met 45 minutes every week from that first kickoff through handoff. The domain focal set one rule at the first session: the meeting was for blockers and decisions, not status. "Red on a chart is good," the domain focal told the group. "It means we're measuring something real." A meeting that closed with everything green was a warning sign, not a success.

Ran every week from Day 1 through handoff. A meeting that ends with "everything is green" was the warning sign the domain focal watched for.

The domain focal opened the RAID log that same week, before any risk had materialized. Six months later, when the domain focal needed to explain a scope change to the sponsor, it took one line in an existing log instead of a reconstruction from memory.

1. Interview the people doing the work

The domain focal brought in the people who actually did the work, not their managers, talking to six admissions counselors over three days, 45 to 60 minutes each, at their desks. The one question that produced almost everything: "Walk me through what you did with the first application you opened this morning." Not "how does the process work," which gets you the official version. The morning question gets you the real one.

What the domain focal actually asked
👥 6 counselors, 3 days⏱ 45–60 min each📍 at their desks
  1. Asked what they did this morning, not how the process works.
  2. Watched them navigate their actual screen, not a whiteboard version.
  3. Noted the workarounds without prompting for them, personal spreadsheets, manual copy-paste, the things people build when the official system doesn't work.
  4. Asked "what do you look at to do this, and where does that come from" at every step, building the data map the architect would need later.
  5. Followed it when one of the counselors mentioned something outside the question guide, a manual cross-check nobody had asked about.
What it surfaced: every counselor independently described the same routine, manually cross-referencing each document in Box against a mental checklist built from experience. Three of the six kept personal tracking spreadsheets. None used the official checklist.
Convergent signal was the finding: when six people independently describe the same workaround, that's not an anecdote, that's the answer.
⚠ Pain point
Mapping the process without mapping the data underneath it
The domain focal asked one more question at every step: "what data would an AI need to do this automatically, and where does it actually live?" That question is what surfaced Box's document permissions varying by type, something a process map alone would have missed entirely, and something that would have been a nasty surprise mid-build instead of a known constraint going in.
2. Map every significant workflow

The domain focal mapped the domain's real workflows — not the org chart version, but the actual work that cost time and money: who did it, hours per week, error rate, what systems it touched. Four candidates surfaced: document verification, outreach prioritization, transcript evaluation, and application processing.

3. Map the data underneath each workflow

At every step in the workflow map, the domain focal and the architect asked: what data would an AI need here, and where does it actually live? That question is what surfaced Box's document permissions varying by document type — a fact a process map alone would have missed entirely, and something that would have been a nasty surprise mid-build if not caught here. It also confirmed that the prospect CRM data was unreliable for any outreach-dependent workflow, which became sequencing information, not a veto.

4. Score each candidate with the Prioritization Matrix

With all four workflows mapped, the domain focal scored each one on five dimensions: value and business impact, technical feasibility, data readiness, strategic fit, and whether a committed sponsor existed. External benchmarks (peer institutions already achieving same-day document verification) anchored the value estimates so the conversation wasn't just about internal conviction.

1. Book the go/no-go meeting before the investigation starts

The domain focal treated this as a two-week investigation into one workflow, not a domain survey. Before it started, the domain focal booked the go/no-go meeting with the sponsor and the architect for Day 12, on their calendars immediately, not "sometime in week two." The sponsor needed to arrive knowing they might hear "stop."

The domain focal led three tracks, the architect led the fourth. All four converged into the Day 12 go/no-go.
2. Run the three spend & volume tracks

The domain focal ran three parallel tracks: spend and volume (14-day review cycle, 60% of counselor time on manual verification, ~120 applications per counselor per month), user conversations (three of six counselors keeping personal spreadsheets because no standardized checklist existed — the same workaround, independently, six times), and peer benchmarks (other institutions with similar document volumes had already moved to same-day verification using AI document processing). Each track alone would have been insufficient; all three together made the recommendation undeniable.

3. Map the as-is flow and root causes

The current-state map produced from the counselor conversations showed the root cause clearly: every counselor was running their own mental checklist, built from experience, against documents stored in Box with no standardization. The bottleneck wasn't volume — it was the absence of a shared standard that could be automated. That insight is what shaped the future-state design: the AI needed to enforce a consistent checklist, not just speed up the counselors' existing process.

4. Validate the technical path

The architect's findings: documents already lived in Box and were reachable via API, an OCR pipeline was buildable on the existing stack, and the one real constraint — Box permissions varying by document type — was a two-week data-access conversation, not a blocker. The architect also flagged the university's ARB process as needing to start in Step 4, not at the end of the sprint. That flag was the difference between launching on Day 90 and discovering a six-week approval gate at Day 80.

5. Lock confirmed baselines and make the go/no-go call

The domain focal locked three baselines confirmed directly from Banner and Box data: 14-day average document review cycle, 60% of counselor time on manual tasks, and 8% outreach conversion rate. These became the KPIs the go/no-go at Day 90 would be judged against — not estimates, not remembered figures, numbers pulled from the actual systems the AI would eventually touch.

⚠ Pain point
Data without insight
The domain focal could have stopped at a technically thorough analysis — spend numbers, a process map, peer benchmarks — and still not known why the workflow failed. The numbers showed where the problem was; the counselor conversations told the domain focal why it persisted. The go/no-go meeting waited until both were answered.

At the Day 12 meeting, the recommendation was proceed. The sponsor approved staffing that same week.

Handing forward to Step 4: a validated opportunity, a go decision, confirmed baseline KPIs, and a technical architect who already understands the data and integration landscape. Solution Design starts with the question answered.

The product owner and the change owner joined here, not at the start of the sprint, because what got decided in Solution Design would directly shape both their jobs. The product owner would write and own the requirements; the change owner started planning who was affected and what they'd need to know.

1. Align on a shared future-state vision

Before anyone wrote a requirement, the domain focal, the architect, and the product owner spent a half-day in the same room with one goal: leave with one mental model of the destination. They walked in with three different versions of what "automated document verification" meant. They walked out with one: a unified AI layer that verifies documents in under two hours, notifies applicants automatically about missing items, and surfaces only exceptions to counselors — eliminating manual review entirely. That shared picture is what made the requirements conversation in Week 2 move three times faster.

2. Run the build vs. buy assessment

The four-track investigation in Step 3 had already answered two of the three lenses: business fit (spend, volume, and user conversations) and technical fit (Box API and OCR feasibility on the existing stack). In Solution Design, the architect ran the third: strategic fit. Would owning this capability internally still make sense in a year? A vendor OCR tool meant recurring licensing cost and lock-in to whatever document types the vendor chose to support next; building meant the team owned the logic and could extend it to transcript evaluation in Wave 2 without renegotiating a contract. All three lenses agreed on build.

OptionCostTime to deployFit
Buy: vendor OCR toolLicensing cost, plus integration workFast, but vendor lock-in on future changesGeneric templates; a poor match for the university's specific document types (transcripts, ID, personal statements)
Build: OCR pipeline on existing stackDevelopment time only, no new licenseFits inside the 90-day windowPurpose-built rules for the exact document types counselors handle; extensible for Wave 2

Build won on all three lenses for this specific decision, not because build is always right. A vendor tool would have been the better call for a more generic, off-the-shelf need with no strategic reason to own it internally.

In scope: document verification and the counselor exception queue. Out of scope, named explicitly — not left implicit: the outreach propensity model, a conversational applicant interface, and transcript evaluation, all three moved to the Wave 2 backlog. The sponsor signed off on both lists before the product owner wrote a single requirement.

4. Write the communications plan and define training

The change owner built the communications plan in Week 1 of Solution Design — not after the build, when the message is an announcement rather than a partnership. The plan defined what each user group would hear, when, and from whom: counselors, counselor managers, IT, and prospective students. The training design followed immediately: what would counselors need to do differently, what scenarios would the training cover, what would count as ready-to-go.

5. Write build-ready requirements

The product owner and the architect wrote requirements in strict order: trigger first, then action, then systems involved, then acceptance criterion. The compare below shows the difference between language that sounds complete and language a developer can actually build from.

✗ Not build-ready
The system should verify documents automatically
Make it easy for counselors to review exceptions
Notifications should go out when documents are missing
The AI should handle the main document types
✓ Build-ready
When a new application is submitted via the portal, the system checks Box for: transcript, government ID, and personal statement. If all three exist and pass format validation, status → "verified." If any are missing, status → "incomplete."
Counselors see only applications with status "exception" (failed validation + flag from OCR). Queue sorted by submitted date. Each row shows: applicant name, missing items, and one-click to view documents.
When status → "incomplete," the applicant receives an email within 15 minutes listing exactly which documents are missing, with upload instructions. Triggered automatically; no counselor action required.

Get all three outputs — vision, scope, and requirements — signed off by the executive sponsor before the build team takes over. That sign-off is what gives the domain focal standing to hold the line when someone tries to add scope mid-sprint.

6. Start the ARB / Path to Production on Day 8

The architect knew the university's architecture review process (ARB) takes real time and the solution can't launch without clearing it, so it was initiated on Day 8 of Solution Design, not after the sprint ended. It cleared before the build team's first day.

The future-state process map from the Week 1 whiteboard session, the shared picture that made the requirements conversation move three times faster.

1. Confirm training is complete before launch day

The change owner ran four training sessions in the two weeks before go-live — one for each cohort of six counselors. Training covered not just how to use the exception queue, but what the AI is actually doing (checking three document types against a validation rule), what it doesn't do (make the admission decision), what to do when a flag looks wrong (escalation path to the change owner), and how to submit feedback. By launch day, every counselor had completed the training and could demonstrate their workflow in the new system. The old manual checklist was retired for the pilot group on Day 1 — not "available if you prefer it," gone.

What happens when people run both processes in parallel: The workaround rate never drops because the old process is always available as the comfort fallback. Retiring the old process explicitly — even for just the first two counselors — is what makes "adoption" mean something. The change owner communicated this clearly: "For your document verification work, the exception queue is the only path as of Monday."
2. Start small: pilot with UAT alumni first

Rather than launch to all 24 admissions counselors at once, the change owner started with the two who'd been active in UAT — already familiar with the tool, already curious. Real applications, no fallback. Two weeks of their real work, with the change owner checking in every other day. Their feedback led to one more interface fix before the broader rollout. More importantly, their result — "I'm clearing twice as many applications in the same time" — traveled through the team on its own, said to colleagues, not published by leadership. That's the proof point that moves hesitant peers.

Starting small and letting a real result do the persuading, rather than a launch announcement.
