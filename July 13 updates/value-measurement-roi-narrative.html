Value Measurement & ROI

Good measurement is not about reporting up. It is about knowing whether what you built is working, having the evidence to prove it, and using that evidence to make the next decision. This chapter helps you define the right metrics for your workflow, track them in a way that is honest and lightweight, and build the proof needed to sustain and grow what you have built.

Why measurement matters

📌 Metrics exist for your domain, not for the program. Pick the 3 to 5 workflow metrics you control and can defend. Organization-wide AI metrics are tracked at the program level and are not your job.

Across IBM domains, the most consistent friction around metrics is the fear that tracking will be used to judge rather than help. The reality is the opposite. The teams that skip measurement are the ones who cannot make the case to keep going, cannot get resources for Wave 2, and cannot prove to leadership that the investment was worth it. Good metrics exist for the domain, not for the program.

Why this matters: whose metrics these are and why they pay off

Teams that define and track their own metrics have a structural advantage when it comes to defending their work, securing continued investment, and knowing when to pivot. For example, the Q2C (Quote-to-Cash) team tracks Effort Reduction and Cycle Time sprint over sprint not because they have to. When the data showed effort reduction climbing from 8% in Sprint 1 to 37% by Sprint 5, that story sold the next phase of the program. Measurement is the evidence you are building.

Two types of metrics exist here. Workflow metrics are the ones you define, track, and own: effort reduction, cycle time, cost per transaction, quality scores, adoption of your specific tool. Organization-wide metrics like overall AI usage rates or AI literacy completion are tracked at the program level. This chapter focuses on the metrics that serve your team.

Productivity
Time per task, steps removed, effort reduction %, hours saved per person per week on this workflow
Quality
Error rate, false pass/fail rate, rework volume, output accuracy, exception rate
Cycle time
End-to-end workflow duration, step-to-step latency, processing speed, time to completion
Adoption
% of eligible users active on your specific tool, frequency of use, retention after launch
Financial
Cost per transaction, vendor spend replaced, headcount redeployment, ROI against development cost
Satisfaction
NPS (Net Promoter Score) with your tool, OSAT (Overall Satisfaction score), user-reported time saved, qualitative feedback on this workflow

The most important thing to know is that you are not limited to a predefined list. The right metrics are the ones that tell a clear story about whether your specific workflow improved. For example, the Q2C team measures False Pass Rate and False Fail Rate because those tell them whether the AI validation agent is accurate. That metric matters for their workflow and would be irrelevant for most others. Pick the 3 to 5 metrics that would make a skeptic believe the transformation worked.

Engage Value Measurement & ROI Steps 1 & 2 · Assess & Analyze

Agree on what success looks like before you build anything.

📌 One focused metrics design session, before any solution is designed, prevents the most common failure: defining metrics after the build to make the results look good.
Who to involve
Domain transformation lead Process SMEs Finance / EBM partner Executive sponsor

The single most valuable measurement activity you can do happens before any solution is designed: running a short metrics design session with your process SMEs and Finance partner. In practice this is a focused conversation, not a workshop with deliverables, where you answer three questions together. What would make this transformation a clear success? What does the current process cost to run? And what is realistic to expect from an AI intervention of this scope?

The output is not a complex model. It is a shared definition of success that the team, the sponsor, and Finance all agree on before a line of requirements is written. This prevents the most common failure mode: defining metrics after the build to make the results look good.

Metrics design session

Agree on what to measure and what good looks like

Run this as a focused conversation with 3 to 5 people: the domain transformation lead, 1 to 2 process SMEs who know the current workflow well, and your Finance or EBM (Enterprise Business Management) partner. The goal is to walk away with a shared answer to each of the following:

What to agree on in the metrics design session
1
What is the primary value this transformation delivers? Pick one: productivity (time and effort), quality (accuracy and errors), cycle time (speed), or cost (spend). This shapes which metrics matter most. Most workflows have one primary and at most one secondary.
2
What does the workflow cost to run today? Get specific with your process SMEs: how long does step A to step B take? How many people touch it? What is the error or rework rate? What is the cost per transaction? These are your baselines. Actual numbers from the platform are better than estimates.
3
What would a meaningful improvement look like? Set a direction before the solution is designed, not after. "We expect 40% effort reduction in this step" is a hypothesis that shapes the build. Get Finance or your EBM partner (the business unit's financial measurement lead) to sense-check whether the numbers make sense and whether they tie to something on the balance sheet.
4
How will you capture the data? Platform telemetry is the best option. If your tool already tracks time-in-step, use it. Manual tracking (time logs, simple spreadsheets) is acceptable. Surveys are a weak fallback because they measure perception, not performance. The data source you choose now is the one you will be held to later.
5
Who owns each metric? Name a person for each one. Metrics without owners do not get tracked.

Frame the financial value in the terms leadership uses. When your results reach a VP or a finance review, they will be compared against every other investment in the portfolio. Presenting them in the shape leadership already thinks in makes your case stronger and saves you a round of translation later. Three habits do most of the work:

  • Classify financial impact as revenue enabled, cost savings, or risk avoidance. These are the three categories finance compares investments in. Every financial metric you define should map to one of them, and if your solution improves more than one enterprise workflow (Quote-to-Cash and Source-to-Pay, say), state the value it generates in each workflow it touches. The same capability can create distinct value in each.
  • Keep a projected figure and a realized figure, separately labeled. The projected value is your estimate at design time, grounded in the baseline and stated assumptions. The realized value is what you later confirm from actual data after deployment. Leadership treats these two numbers very differently, and blending them is how measurement credibility is lost. Record the projected value now, in the metrics design session, and plan for how the realized value will be confirmed.
  • Anchor targets against reference points that hold up. Across IBM transformations, two useful markers: around two hours saved per person per week, and 60% or more of intended users adopting the tool within six months of launch. If your targets sit far below these, it is worth asking whether the workflow was worth transforming.
⚠ Pain point
Numbers that do not survive finance scrutiny
Three habits protect your numbers. Count people once: if the same person benefits from two initiatives, do not sum them into one impact figure. Label volume honestly: training completions are not trained people, and task counts are not users; say which one you are reporting. And where the data cannot support a precise number yet, say "data collection in progress" rather than manufacturing a percentage. A smaller, defensible number builds more trust than a bigger one that falls apart under one follow-up question.

The best baseline data comes from inside the platform people already use. If your team already has a system that tracks how long each step takes, that data is the baseline. Building your measurement approach around instrumentation that already exists is far more reliable than setting up new tracking after the fact.

Each metric needs all five columns filled before the build starts. The Reasoning column is the most important: if you cannot state clearly why this metric matters for this workflow, it is not the right metric. Use the same source for baseline and post-launch measurement so the comparison holds.
⚠ Pain point
Skipping the metrics conversation because it feels premature
Most teams put off the metrics conversation because the solution does not exist yet and it feels speculative. The result is that they arrive at the end of the build with no agreed baseline, no defined target, and a team that starts negotiating what success means after the fact. The metrics design session does not require a complete solution. It requires a clear enough understanding of the workflow to say what "better" looks like. That exists at Engage.
Case StudyMCC Event Speaker Agent: defining metrics before building

The MCC team ran their metrics conversation during Workflow Analysis before any design work started. They measured the current manual process with their event planning SMEs: average time to identify and confirm speakers was 5 days, the average number of back-and-forth emails per event was 12, and the speaker confirmation rate was 65%. They set targets before the build: 2 days, 3 emails, 85% confirmation rate. Finance confirmed the metrics tied to G&A reduction through event planning efficiency. They tracked weekly using a spreadsheet linked to their event management system. When the solution launched, the baseline was already there. No scrambling to reconstruct what things looked like before.

Who to involve
Domain transformation lead Finance / EBM partner Product owner CIO/BPT architect (for instrumentation)

Discovery is where the candidate metrics from Engage get confirmed against real data and locked in. Step 3

Workflow Analysis: confirm baselines from source data

The Workflow Analysis gives you access to the actual numbers behind the process. Use this time to capture baselines from platform data rather than estimates. If the workflow lives inside a system, that system often already tracks the things you want to measure: time in step, transaction count, error flags, completion rates. Those numbers are the baseline.

For each metric, answer: what is the actual current value, where does that number come from, and can we capture it the same way after the solution launches? The before-and-after comparison is only credible if both halves come from the same source. A baseline from a survey compared to a post-launch number from platform telemetry is not a valid comparison.

The most defensible measurement is a before-and-after comparison on the same metric from the same source. If you track step-time in the current system, track step-time in the AI-enabled system. That is the evidence that survives scrutiny.
⚠ Pain point
The baseline does not exist when the build ends
The most consistent tracking failure across domains is launching a solution, seeing meaningful improvement, and then not being able to prove how much because no one captured what things looked like before. Sometimes the old tool is decommissioned. Sometimes the process has already changed. Sometimes no one thought to take a snapshot. Capture the baseline in Workflow Analysis, before anything changes. One hour of data collection now saves months of reconstruction later. In many cases, the reconstruction is not possible at all.
Step 4

Solution Design: make tracking a requirement, not an afterthought

Tracking should be in the requirements document from the first version. For each metric you defined in Engage and confirmed in Workflow Analysis, the requirements specify what the solution must capture, how it captures it, and where that data goes. If the product owner and architect cannot tell you how Hours Saved will be measured in the MVP, that is a gap in the requirements, not a detail to figure out after launch.

The specific instrumentation needed depends on the workflow. Some solutions log usage and completion data automatically through the platform. Others need explicit telemetry: tracking which step was completed, by whom, and how long it took. Work with the CIO/BPT architect to confirm what is possible given the systems in scope. If EBM integration (such as Apptio) is planned for financial reporting, that data pipeline needs to be scoped in design, not wired up after the solution goes live.

Where EBM fits. For CIO-driven projects (via Path to Production / OnboardAI), EBM is partnering with the CIO AI Platform team on value measurement upfront, so the tracking is scoped here in Solution Design and runs throughout the 90-day sprint. For fully domain or business-unit-led AI projects, more is coming on EBM value measurement. In the meantime, the approach in this chapter (define the metrics, capture the baseline, build tracking into the solution) is what makes your data ready to plug into EBM reporting when that guidance lands.

⚠ Pain point
Building the solution and then trying to add tracking
Instrumentation is far harder to add after the solution is built than to include from the start. A solution that did not collect usage data from Sprint 1 has a gap in its pilot evidence. A solution that does not log time-in-step has to rely on surveys to estimate productivity impact. Neither is as credible as data captured automatically throughout the build. Make telemetry a requirement in the MVP. If it cannot be built in, decide now whether you are comfortable with the measurement approach you will be left with.
What to specify in Solution Design for each metric
1
What event triggers a data point? User completes a step, AI returns a result, a transaction closes. Be specific about what gets recorded and when.
2
Where is the data stored? The platform's native logs, a custom table, Apptio, EBM. Name the system. If it does not exist yet, name who will build it and when.
3
Who on the build team owns the instrumentation? Assign it like any other requirement. Instrumentation without an owner does not get built.
4
When will you validate the data pipeline? Sprint 1 is the target. If the telemetry is not working by the end of Sprint 1, you are not capturing the pilot evidence you need.
5
If MVP scope changes, update the metrics. A metric tied to a feature that was de-scoped is a metric you cannot report. Keep the metric list aligned with what is being built.

Case StudyQ2C Order Validation: tracking per sprint from day one

The Q2C team built measurement into the solution design for every sprint. In the requirements, each use case had defined inputs, pass/fail criteria, exception taxonomies, and evidence requirements. The Apptio decision dashboard was scoped as part of the build, not added later. By the time Sprint 1 ended, they had live data on Effort Reduction, Cycle Time, MVP Coverage, False Pass Rate, and False Fail Rate. Sprint by sprint, effort reduction grew: 8.69% in Sprint 1, 7.65% in Sprint 2, 16.39% in Sprint 3, 22.88% in Sprint 4, 37.77% in Sprint 5. That trajectory was only possible because the tracking was in place from the first sprint, not reconstructed at the end.

Execute Value Measurement & ROI Steps 5 & 6 · Experimentation · Scale & Adopt

Track sprint over sprint. Use the data to make the go/no-go decision and what comes next.

📌 Measured actuals from real work, not projections, are what make the go/no-go credible. A flat metric in Sprint 2 is information to act on, not a failure to hide.
Who to involve
Domain transformation lead Product owner Finance / EBM partner Business owner (after Day 90) Executive sponsor
Step 5

Experimentation: track weekly, report at steering, use data to pivot

Track your metrics every two weeks, aligned to sprint cycles. Bring the numbers to the steering committee, not as a status report but as the basis for decisions. If Effort Reduction is flat at 8% after two sprints and the target is 40%, that is not a failure. It is information. Either the approach needs to change, the feature set needs adjustment, or the target needs to be recalibrated with Finance. Catching that in Sprint 2 is very different from catching it at Day 90.

Have real users do real work with the imperfect MVP and measure the actual difference. Not a demo. Not a simulation. Actual tasks from actual work, with the time or effort captured through the tool. That evidence ("this step took 45 minutes before and takes 8 minutes now") is what makes the go/no-go recommendation credible. Projections and estimates do not survive leadership scrutiny the same way measured actuals do.

Example: Q2C effort reduction sprint over sprint. The trajectory across five sprints (8% to 38%) made the case for the next phase of the program. This story only exists because the tracking was in place from Sprint 1.
⚠ Pain point
Only measuring at Day 90 instead of sprint over sprint
Teams that only look at metrics at the Day 90 go/no-go meeting miss the most valuable use of data during the build: course correction. If effort reduction is not improving sprint over sprint, something in the approach needs to change. The earlier you see that, the more time you have to address it. Weekly or bi-weekly tracking during the build is not administrative overhead. It is the mechanism that keeps the build focused on the outcome rather than on feature delivery.
Step 5 continued

The Day 90 go/no-go decision

The Day 90 decision is a business decision, not a technical one. The question is whether the MVP demonstrated enough value, at enough reliability, to justify scaling the investment. Bring the actual metrics from the pilot, compared to the baseline and target agreed in Engage, to the steering committee. Report the financial result in both forms: the projected value you scoped in Engage and the realized value measured from the pilot, clearly labeled. Let the numbers drive the recommendation.

A go decision at Day 90 does not mean all metrics hit their target. It means the trajectory is credible, the approach is working, and the investment in Wave 2 is justified. A no-go decision is not a failure. It is the measurement working as intended. It is far better to discover that an approach does not deliver the expected value at Day 90 than at month 18 when far more has been invested.

Step 6

Scale & Adopt: sustain tracking after launch and use it to grow

After a go decision, the tracking does not stop. It shifts from sprint cadence to monthly reporting. The business owner takes over the KPI dashboard. Finance validates the realized value. The data becomes the evidence base for scaling: it is what you use to justify the Wave 2 roadmap to leadership, to secure additional capacity, and to tell the story of what the transformation accomplished.

Keep your AI System record current. If your solution was built through IBM's Path to Production process, it has an official AI System record with business value details attached. Update that record as realized value comes in. It is how your solution's impact stays visible to leadership after launch without you re-presenting it, and it is what keeps the solution supported and resourced.

The teams that have sustained measurement after launch are the ones who have been able to argue for more resources, expand the solution to new users or workflows, and build genuine organizational capability around AI-enabled work. The teams that stopped tracking after the go decision often found that executive support drifted because there was no ongoing evidence of impact.

How to sustain value tracking after launch
1
Hand off the KPI dashboard to the business owner before the build team steps back. Monthly reporting on the same metrics tracked during the pilot. No new framework, no new system.
2
Validate hard dollar savings with Finance quarterly. Not estimated: confirmed. This is what makes the business case defensible when someone asks how the investment performed.
3
Use the data to prioritize the backlog. If effort reduction plateaued at 38% and the target is 70%, what feature in Wave 2 closes the gap? Let the metric data drive sequencing, not gut feel about what is most interesting to build.
4
Build the ROI narrative for the next phase. The data from this workflow (the trajectory, the actuals against baseline, the Finance-validated savings) is the business case for Wave 2. Document it in the ROI Analysis before the team moves on.

Case StudyMCC Speaker Agent: sustained tracking after launch

After the Speaker Agent launched, the MCC team tracked adoption metrics weekly and found that while 85% of event planners had tried the agent, only 60% were using it regularly. Without that data, the team would have declared success based on the initial adoption number. With it, they diagnosed the gap: a specific set of edge cases was routing planners back to the manual process. They fixed it, ran targeted training, and within six weeks adoption was above 80%. The ongoing tracking, not the launch metric, is what drove sustained value.

The ROI analysis at the six-month mark showed confirmed time savings of 2.8 days per event on speaker confirmation workflows, down from 5 days, with speaker confirmation rate up to 81%. Those numbers, validated by Finance, were the foundation for the business case that secured resources for the next phase of the MCC transformation.
