How-to Deep Dive

Prioritization & Roadmap

Many AI transformations don't fail because of the technology. They fail because the wrong problem was chosen, too many things were attempted at once, or no one made a clear decision to stop when the evidence said to. This chapter covers how to identify the right workflows to pursue, evaluate them rigorously with both business and technical SMEs, build a plan that's actually deliverable, and keep the roadmap honest as you learn.

This chapter follows the same six-step transformation process used across every domain chapter (Engage through Execute) and shows what prioritization and roadmap work happens at each step.

Engage Step 1 · Tech & Data Assessment Prioritization & Roadmap

Before you can pick the right workflow, you need to know what you're working with.

Step 1 builds the baseline that every prioritization decision depends on.

  • The first snapshot is a picture of your domain as it exists today: the current state across people, processes, technology, and data.
  • The second is a quantified case for why any of this is worth changing.

Without both, you're asking leadership to bet on a feeling rather than a number. Begin by gathering the current-state picture with process and operational SMEs, then working with your finance or EBM partner to attach value estimates to the domain's major pain areas.

Step 1 produces two artifacts:

  • the Current State Assessment, which captures the domain's people, processes, technology, data, and operating model (the honest "where we are today" snapshot); and
  • Opportunity Sizing, which quantifies what it would be worth to change, tied to the balance sheet, G&A reduction, or revenue impact.
Step 1

How to conduct the Current State Assessment

The Current State Assessment is not a research project. It's a structured conversation with the people who run the domain. The goal is a clear, shared picture of how the domain actually operates today, documented well enough that someone outside the domain can understand it. You're capturing four things:

1
People and roles. Who are the personas doing the work? How many of them? What does a day look like for each role? Document the team structure, key decision-makers, and who touches each major workflow. This becomes the basis for every "who does what" question in prioritization.
2
Processes and workflows. What are the major workflows in the domain, end to end? For each one: what triggers it, what are the steps, who is responsible at each step, how long does it take, and where does it break? This doesn't need to be exhaustive. You're capturing enough to identify where the biggest costs and friction are concentrated.
3
Technology and tools. What systems does the team use today? Which are connected, and which require manual hand-offs between them? Where does data live, and who controls access? The tech inventory here feeds directly into the feasibility assessment in Step 2.
4
Operating model and readiness. How does the domain make decisions? Who has authority to approve changes to processes or tools? Are there compliance requirements, data governance constraints, or active IT initiatives that would affect an AI build? These are the constraints that shape what's actually possible.

The right sources for this are the domain focal, 2–3 process owners who can speak to how work actually runs (not just how it's documented), and the tech or data lead who knows the systems. A half-day working session with the right people, structured around these four areas, is typically enough to produce a first draft. Validate it back with participants before moving on.

For Opportunity Sizing: once you have the current-state picture, work with your finance or EBM partner to translate the friction you've documented into dollar or hour estimates. What does it cost to run each major workflow today? What's the error or rework rate? How much time do people spend on work that AI could do? These numbers don't need to be exact. A directional estimate is enough to rank candidates. The point is to shift the conversation from "which workflow is most painful" to "which workflow is most expensive."

Steps 1 and 2 together produce one output: a scored, ranked list of candidate workflows with one clear winner. This is the Prioritization & Roadmap deliverable from Engage.

Engage Step 2 · Business Process Mapping Prioritization & Roadmap

Build the list. Score it honestly. Pick one.

The goal of Step 2 is to create a short, scored list of real potential areas for AI investment. This list will be comprehensive, covering every significant process in your domain and all the technology and data foundations those processes rely on. With the help of process SMEs who can tell you what the work actually costs and where it breaks, and tech SMEs who can tell you whether the data exists to support an AI intervention and how hard it would be to build, you will assess opportunity value, technical feasibility, and data readiness for each candidate. You can't score without both in the room. Begin by mapping every significant workflow with process SMEs, then assess value against the baseline from Step 1, then pressure-test feasibility with a tech SME, then run the full set through the Prioritization Matrix.

Step 2

Process inventory → assess value → assess feasibility → prioritize

Step 1: Build a process inventory. Work with process SMEs to list every significant workflow in the domain. Don't filter yet. For each one, document: who does it, how many hours per week it consumes, what the error or rework rate looks like, and what downstream work it blocks or enables. You're building a raw inventory, not a ranked list. If a workflow isn't on the list, it cannot be selected later.

Step 2: Assess opportunity value. For each workflow in the inventory, assess what it would be worth to transform it. Our AIFT methodology measures value in three forms:

  • Time saved (hours freed up per person);
  • Cost reduced (direct budget impact, error rework eliminated); or
  • Revenue enabled (faster cycle times unlocking deals or outcomes).

Your process SMEs should provide the baseline numbers, drawing on the Current State Assessment from Step 1; your finance or EBM partner validates them. A rough assessment is appropriate here. You're ranking candidates, not finalizing a business case.

Step 3: Assess technical feasibility and automation potential. For each high-value candidate, bring in a tech SME to pressure-test it against your Technology & Data Inventory. Three questions:

  • Is the data that an AI would need accessible and structured enough to use?
  • Are there integration constraints or governance requirements that would significantly increase complexity?
  • Could a meaningful solution be built and delivered, or would significant infrastructure work be needed first?

The answers determine whether a high-value process is buildable now, or whether it belongs in the "invest to unblock" category.

Step 4: Use the Prioritization Matrix to score and rank. With the process inventory, value assessments, and feasibility ratings in hand, run every candidate through a consistent scoring rubric. The matrix is what makes your recommendation defensible: it shows leadership the math behind the selection, not just the conclusion. When a leader pushes back, you have data to point to.

Why projects get mis-prioritized

Poor prioritization almost always starts from poor analysis. The starting point differs, but the outcome is the same: the wrong project gets built.

Starting with what people complain about
Ignores value and technical feasibility. Builds something that alleviates friction but doesn't move the needle. Risk: low-ROI delivery.
Starting with a senior leader's idea
May lack a clear goal, measurable outcome, or a realistic path to building it. The problem isn't the seniority. It's the absence of analysis. Risk: project without success criteria.
Chasing a technically interesting project
Exciting to build; may not address a real business cost or bottleneck. A solution in search of a problem. Risk: successful build, no one cares about scaling it.
Targeting a system replacement or overhaul
Scope is too large for a 90-day test, and ROI is diffuse or long-dated. Risk: cost without near-term validation.
Picking the highest-value workflow without checking sponsor support
Even the right choice fails without an executive who will show up, clear blockers, and defend scope. Risk: stalls at the first obstacle.
The fix is the same in every case: use a consistent scoring rubric for every candidate, show the math, and let the numbers drive the conversation. Your recommendation is only as strong as the evidence behind it.

Also look outside your organization: what are comparable companies doing with similar processes? Benchmarks tell you what's actually achievable, so you're aiming at a real target rather than an improvement to a broken baseline, and they give leadership a concrete frame for why this investment makes sense.

Each candidate gets plotted based on its scores. The top-right quadrant is where you start. This chart is your recommendation to leadership. The scoring behind it is what makes it defensible when someone pushes back.

Step 3

Workflow Analysis

Prove the opportunity is real before committing to a build.

The Workflow Analysis is a time-boxed investigation (typically two weeks) into the workflow you selected. You're not designing anything yet — you're gathering enough evidence to make a confident go/no-go recommendation to your steering committee.

  • You need process SMEs who know the current state in detail, and a tech SME who can assess data access and integration paths as you go.
  • You're gathering: how much the workflow costs to run today, what the key performance indicators are, what best-in-class looks like at peer organizations, and what's actually causing it to perform poorly.
  • Your output is a recommendation: proceed to Solution Design, or return to the prioritized list.

Your executive sponsor's commitment from Engage now has a concrete meaning: they are providing access to the process owners and SMEs you need. Without that access — real conversations with the people who do the work, not documentation reviews — the Workflow Analysis will not be grounded in reality.

⚠ Pain point
The go/no-go decision that never gets made
The hardest moment in this phase isn't the analysis. It's having an honest conversation about whether to proceed. Teams naturally want to move forward. Sponsors have staked credibility on their selection. The mechanism: book the go/no-go meeting with the steering committee before the analysis starts, and brief the sponsor that they may hear a recommendation to stop. A no-go isn't a failure. It means the investigation worked. Without a hard date and explicit setup, the meeting drifts, the decision softens, and a marginal workflow goes into design and becomes a failed build months later.
The Workflow Analysis follows four investigative tracks that converge on a single go/no-go question. Both outcomes are valid. Document either one and move accordingly.

Step 4

Solution Design: define the vision, scope the build

Solution Design brings in the product owner alongside the process and tech SMEs already in the room.

  • The core P&R deliverable here is the Strategic Roadmap. It defines what's in scope for the MVP, what's explicitly parked as the backlog, and how scope decisions will be managed when pressure builds mid-sprint.

Together you answer three questions in sequence: you can't scope the MVP until you have the end-state vision, and you can't write requirements until you have the scope.

First: define the end-state vision. Describe the fully transformed workflow. What would it look like if redesigned from scratch with today's AI capabilities? This isn't a product spec; it's a shared picture of where you're ultimately heading. Every scope decision after this should be tested against it: does this move us toward that vision, or away from it?

Second: define the MVP scope, and the explicit out-of-scope list. The MVP is the smallest version of the solution that meaningfully tests whether the core hypothesis is right. Define it with two lists: what ships in the first sprint, and what is explicitly parked. The out-of-scope list is as important as the in-scope list: everything not explicitly parked becomes an implicit yes, which is how timelines slip. The out-of-scope list is also the seed of your post-MVP roadmap.

Third: write build-ready requirements and confirm the timeline. The test: give a developer the requirements document and ask if they can write a user story from it without asking you a single clarifying question. If they can't, the requirements aren't ready. This is also the right moment to confirm that what's in scope can actually be delivered within the sprint window. Scope that can't ship in the planned timeframe needs to be flagged and re-evaluated now, not mid-sprint.

Each week ends with a concrete milestone. This is what you hand to the build team alongside the requirements.
Five parallel workstreams across the 90-day sprint. The go/no-go gate at Day 90 is fixed. Scope adjusts to meet it, not the other way around. Both roadmaps are produced in Solution Design and handed to the build team before Sprint 1 begins.
⚠ Pain point
Handing over requirements no one can build from
The most common handoff failure in this entire process happens here. Business teams write requirements that make sense internally but are too conceptual for a development team to act on. The test is simple: give a developer the requirements document and ask if they can write a user story from it without asking you a single clarifying question. If they can't, it's not ready.

Get the scope (both lists) signed off by the executive sponsor before the build team takes over. That sign-off is what gives the project lead standing to hold the line when someone asks to add scope mid-sprint.

Execute Steps 5 & 6 · Experimentation · Scale & Adopt Prioritization & Roadmap
Step 5

Experimentation

Hold the scope. Measure honestly. Decide at Day 90.

The roadmap you built in Discover now runs against reality. The P&R work doesn't end when the build starts. It directly shapes two critical decisions: the Day 90 go/no-go, and what gets prioritized in the next wave. Both require the same discipline as the initial prioritization: evidence over enthusiasm, math over momentum.

During the sprint, prioritization pressure comes from a different direction: not "what should we build?" but "can we add this to the current sprint?" The answer, almost always, should be no, or at minimum, "what comes out to make room?" The roadmap signed off in Solution Design is the shield that makes that response defensible. Without it, scope creep is inevitable and the Day 90 decision becomes meaningless.

Scope reductions during the build are not failures. They're the mechanism that makes 90-day delivery work. What determines whether a de-scope preserves or destroys executive confidence is how it's communicated: show what was cut, why, when it comes back, and what the team is protecting by cutting it.

⚠ Pain point
Go/no-go criteria defined after the sprint ends
If success criteria aren't agreed before Day 1, they'll be defined after the sprint, which means they'll be defined to pass. The Day 90 decision needs to answer a specific, pre-agreed question: did this MVP demonstrate enough value, at enough reliability, to justify scaling the investment? Write that question down before the sprint begins. A metric without a pre-agreed threshold is not a decision rule. It's a number that will be interpreted however the moment requires.
Step 6

Scale & Adopt: update the roadmap, prioritize what's next

A go at Day 90 doesn't mean "everything." It means this MVP, with these users, showed enough signal to continue. The post-MVP roadmap, seeded in Solution Design when you created the out-of-scope list, now needs to be activated and reprioritized based on what was actually learned. Use the same scoring framework from Engage: what did you learn about data quality or integration complexity that changes feasibility scores? What did users tell you during UAT that shifts value estimates? The Prioritization Matrix is a living tool, updated with evidence at every gate.

Before the transformation team steps back, two ownership roles need to be named explicitly: a business owner accountable for adoption, user support, and ongoing business outcomes, and a technical lead who maintains the infrastructure, manages updates, and handles system-level issues. When these roles aren't named before handoff, the solution degrades quietly.

Naming owners is necessary but not sufficient. You also need a shared definition of "done": what does full adoption look like, what does the full-featured product look like relative to the end-state vision from Step 4, and what adoption KPI marks success? These definitions shape the post-90-day roadmap and give the business owner something to manage toward.

The go/no-go at Day 90 starts the clock on the post-launch roadmap, not the end of the project. Define what "fully complete" means before handoff so the business owner has a concrete target to manage toward.
1
Confirm project clarity before Day 1: end-state vision, MVP scope, SME contacts, operating model, and how mid-sprint requirements changes flow to the backlog.
2
Lock go/no-go metrics and data source before writing any code. The Day 90 decision needs a pre-agreed question and answer standard.
3
Test the riskiest assumption first. Whatever would most likely invalidate the whole approach goes into Sprint 1.
4
Run weekly steering with demos and blockers, not status updates. Mid-sprint requirement changes get logged and triaged, not absorbed silently or rejected reflexively.
5
Name a business owner and a technical lead before handoff. Joint responsibility for user support and change management. No single owner = no accountability.
6
Define "fully complete" (MECE users, full feature set, adoption target) and build the 30/90/180-day post-launch roadmap before the transformation team exits.

Case StudyState University: Day 90 and what came next

The go/no-go criteria were set before Sprint 1: document verification time under 4 hours for 90% of applications, counselor time on manual verification reduced by at least 50%, and zero increase in error rate. At Day 90, the data showed: average verification time 1.8 hours, counselor time on manual work down 64%, error rate unchanged. The recommendation was clear: proceed.

The Strategic Roadmap was updated the same week: Wave 2 activated the outreach prioritization workflow from the parked list. The team ran the Prioritization Matrix again on the remaining backlog, this time with updated data access scores based on what they'd learned about the Box integration. The next workflow entered Discover three weeks after Day 90.
