Adoption & Change

Drive real usage and behavior change through user involvement, UAT, communications, enablement, and adoption tracking. This chapter helps domain leads move from building a solution to ensuring it's actually used and delivers value. The goal is not to deploy technology; the goal is to change how people work and prove that the new way is better.

Engage Tech & Data Assessment · Business Process Mapping

Start change management on day one. Not after the build.

Most teams treat change management as something that happens after the technology is built — a training session, a rollout plan, maybe some communications. By then, it's too late. The people who will use the solution weren't involved in designing it. No one mapped how their day-to-day work will actually change. And when adoption stalls, there's no plan to intervene because no one was tracking it in the first place.

Change management starts in Engage, not Execute. Before you write a line of code, you need to know: who is affected by this transformation, how their roles will change, and whether they're likely to resist or champion it. That early read shapes everything — the solution design, the adoption plan, the communications strategy, and most importantly, whether you staff a dedicated change owner or try to bolt it on later.

Map every affected stakeholder by impact and influence. Champions go into your UAT pool and communications plan. High-risk resistors need direct sponsor engagement. Don't wait until Execute to discover who can block adoption.
⚠ Pain point
Treating change management as a post-build activity
The most common failure pattern: the team builds a technically sound solution, then realizes two weeks before launch that no one planned training, identified UAT users, or mapped how the new process actually works. By then, there's no time to do it right. The fix is simple but requires discipline: fund a dedicated change owner in Engage, give them a seat at the steering committee, and make adoption planning a required deliverable before Solution Design is approved.

The other critical decision in Engage is whether you have visible, active executive sponsorship. Not someone who agreed to put their name on a charter — someone who will show up to steering meetings, clear blockers, and personally message the organization about why this matters. If that person doesn't exist or won't commit, adoption will stall the moment it hits resistance. Secure that commitment now, or don't start.

1
Identify affected personas and map them by impact and influence. Know who will champion, resist, or stay neutral.
2
Staff a dedicated change owner — not a PM wearing two hats. Fund it as a real workstream with a seat at steering.
3
Confirm executive sponsor commitment — weekly steering attendance, blocker clearing, and visible organizational messaging.
4
Draft the RACI with one Accountable for adoption. If it's shared or unclear, fix it before kickoff.

Discover Workflow Analysis · Solution Design

Plan adoption before you build. Recruit real users, not managers.

Discover is where most teams make the mistake that kills adoption later: they design the technology without redesigning the work around it. They focus on what the AI will do, but not on what the user will do differently, what training they'll need, how the old process gets retired, or who will own the prompts after launch. The result is a solution that works in a demo but doesn't fit into anyone's actual day.

The fix requires two things. First, re-envision the future-state process — not just the technology, but the end-to-end workflow: what steps people take, what decisions they make, where the AI fits in, and what happens when it's wrong. That future-state becomes the foundation for your adoption plan. Second, recruit real UAT users now — the people who will actually use the solution for their work, not their managers. If you wait until Execute to find them, you'll end up testing with whoever is available, and that's rarely the right group.

Adoption planning is not a post-build activity. By the time you reach Execute, you should already know who your UAT users are, what the training approach is, how communications will roll out, and how the old process gets retired. Discover is when you plan all of it.
⚠ Pain point
Testing with managers instead of real users
When teams wait until Execute to recruit UAT users, they end up with whoever is available — which is usually managers, not the people who actually do the work. Managers test differently. They focus on edge cases and policy compliance, not on whether the solution fits into their daily workflow. The result is a solution that passes UAT but doesn't get adopted, because the real users were never involved. Fix this in Discover: identify 5-10 frontline users, get their managers to commit their time, and bring them into the design process early.

The other critical piece is the communications plan. Not a single launch email — a multi-month arc that starts before the build and continues through scale. Early communications set expectations and build excitement. Mid-build communications show progress and reinforce the "why." Post-launch communications celebrate wins, share user stories, and remind people that the old way is retired. If you don't plan this in Discover, it won't happen in Execute.

⚠ Pain point
No plan to retire the old process
The fastest way to kill adoption is to leave the old process in place. Users will default to what they know, especially when the new solution is imperfect. The fix requires a hard cutover: on a specific date, the old templates are removed, the old system access is revoked, and the only way forward is the new way. That decision needs executive sponsor approval, and it needs to be planned in Discover — not discovered as a problem in Execute when adoption plateaus.
1
Re-envision the future-state process — not just the tech, but the end-to-end workflow: what people do, what decisions they make, where AI fits.
2
Recruit 5-10 real UAT users — frontline workers, not managers. Get their managers to commit their time before the build starts.
3
Draft the communications plan — a multi-month arc from pre-build through scale, not a single launch email.
4
Plan the training approach — office hours, videos, champions, or formal sessions. Decide now, not in Execute.
5
Get sponsor approval for old-process retirement — a hard cutover date when the old way is no longer available.

Case StudyState University — Planning adoption before the build

Before starting the document verification AI build, the enrollment team spent two weeks in Discover planning adoption. They recruited 8 counselors as UAT users (not managers), designed the future-state process showing how counselors would review AI-flagged documents instead of manually checking everything, and drafted a 12-week communications plan. They also got VP approval to retire the old manual checklist on launch day — no fallback option.

When the build started, UAT users were already engaged. By sprint 3, they were testing with real documents. By launch, 85% of counselors had seen demos and knew the old process was ending. Adoption hit 90% in week one because the change was planned before the code was written.

Industry-Proven Change Management Frameworks

Successful adoption doesn't happen by accident. It follows proven patterns. Three frameworks provide complementary lenses for planning and executing change: Prosci ADKAR for individual change, Kotter's 8-Step for organizational transformation, and McKinsey 7-S for systemic alignment. Use them together to diagnose gaps, plan interventions, and sustain adoption.

Framework 1

Prosci ADKAR — The 5 Stages of Individual Change

ADKAR is an acronym for the five outcomes an individual must achieve for change to succeed: Awareness of the need for change, Desire to participate and support it, Knowledge of how to change, Ability to implement new skills, and Reinforcement to sustain the change. Each stage builds on the previous one — you can't skip steps.

ADKAR provides a diagnostic framework: if adoption stalls, identify which stage is incomplete. Most failures occur at Desire (lack of motivation) or Ability (insufficient practice with real work).
MCC Lesseps ExampleADKAR in Practice

Awareness: MCC framed the need as "manual, fragmented pre-event work creates bottlenecks that constrain speed and consistency."

Desire: Leaders modeled AI-supported workflows and positioned AI as a "fast collaborator, not an authority" to build trust.

Knowledge: Embedded learning in workflows (short guidance, examples) rather than formal training courses.

Ability: UAT users worked with real events, not sandbox testing, building competence through practice.

Reinforcement: Post-MVP behaviors (orchestration over execution, criteria-based decisions, learning signals) sustained the change.

Framework 2

Kotter's 8-Step — Organizational Change Roadmap

John Kotter's 8-Step model provides a roadmap for leading organizational change. Unlike ADKAR (which focuses on individuals), Kotter addresses the organizational systems, leadership behaviors, and momentum-building required for large-scale transformation. The steps are sequential but overlapping — you don't finish one before starting the next.

1
Create Urgency — Help others see the need through a bold, aspirational opportunity. MCC positioned itself as "client zero" for AI, supporting strategic shift to net-new clients.
2
Build a Guiding Coalition — Assemble a group with enough power to lead the change. MCC required explicit executive sponsor commitment for go/no-go decisions.
3
Form a Strategic Vision — Create a clear vision to direct the effort. MCC's vision: "orchestration over execution" and "criteria-based over relationship-driven."
4
Communicate the Vision — Use every vehicle to communicate. MCC used persona-specific engagement (Event Owners, Managers, Leadership) with tailored messaging.
5
Enable Action — Remove obstacles and empower people. MCC identified barriers (trust calibration, incomplete inputs) and provided targeted enablement.
6
Generate Short-Term Wins — Create visible successes to build momentum. MCC's UAT participants became champions, sharing early wins.
7
Sustain Acceleration — Use credibility to change systems that don't fit. MCC retired the old process completely and transitioned ownership to the domain.
8
Institute Change — Anchor new approaches in culture. MCC defined post-MVP continuity behaviors to prevent regression to old ways of working.

The most common failure point is Step 7 — teams declare victory too early, before the change is embedded in daily operations. MCC addressed this by defining explicit post-MVP behaviors (orchestration, criteria-based decisions, learning signals) and making domain ownership transition a formal deliverable.

Framework 3

McKinsey 7-S — Systemic Alignment Diagnostic

The McKinsey 7-S Framework assesses whether seven organizational elements are aligned to support change: Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Skills, Style, and Staff. Misalignment in any element creates friction. Use this as a diagnostic: if adoption stalls, check which S is out of sync.

Use 7-S as a diagnostic when adoption stalls: Are roles clear (Structure)? Do workflows support the new way (Systems)? Do people have the right capabilities (Skills)? Is leadership modeling the change (Style)?

Execute Experimentation · Scale & Adopt

Drive trust through real usage. Monitor adoption and intervene when it plateaus.

Execute is where adoption either happens or doesn't. The technology might work perfectly, but if people don't trust it, don't understand it, or can revert to the old way, they won't use it. The goal in Execute is not to wait until launch to start adoption — it's to build trust incrementally through real usage, starting with an imperfect MVP and a small group of users who are willing to work through the rough edges.

That means UAT is not a testing phase — it's the first wave of adoption. You're not asking users to validate requirements in a sandbox. You're asking them to use the solution for real work, with real consequences, and give you feedback on what breaks. That shift — from "test this" to "use this" — is what separates successful adoptions from failed ones. Users who adopt early become your champions. Users who only see a polished demo at launch become skeptics.

Adoption typically follows an S-curve: slow at first (UAT), rapid after launch, then a plateau. The plateau is where most transformations fail — users revert to old habits, edge cases aren't addressed, and no one intervenes. Monitor adoption weekly and have a plan to intervene when it stalls.
⚠ Pain point
Waiting for the solution to be perfect before anyone uses it
Teams naturally want to polish the solution before putting it in front of users. But that delay kills adoption. By the time users see it, they have no context for why it works the way it does, no investment in its success, and no patience for imperfection. The fix is counterintuitive: get a small group using an imperfect MVP for real work as early as sprint 3 or 4. They'll surface the real issues, build trust through involvement, and become your champions at launch. Perfection is the enemy of adoption.

The other critical piece is explainability. Users need to understand not just what the AI did, but why it did it and when to trust it. That means building confidence scores, showing the AI's reasoning, and being explicit about when human judgment is still required. Without explainability, users either blindly trust the AI (dangerous) or don't trust it at all (adoption failure). Build explainability into the solution from the start, not as a post-launch add-on.

Step 6

Scale & Adopt — Transition ownership and sustain adoption

Once the solution launches, someone needs to own it. If you hand off the technology but not the change management, the training, and the ongoing adoption effort, users will revert to old habits the moment they hit a problem. The fix requires explicit handoff: identify a domain owner, train them on how to update prompts and content, and give them a playbook for handling adoption plateaus.

The other critical activity is adoption monitoring and intervention. Track usage weekly — not just "how many people logged in," but "how many people are using it for real work instead of the old process." When adoption plateaus or drops, intervene immediately: run office hours, have champions reach out to holdouts, create examples for edge cases, or have the sponsor send a message. Adoption doesn't sustain itself. It requires active management.

⚠ Pain point
Adoption plateaus as users revert to old templates
The most common post-launch failure: adoption hits 60-70%, then plateaus or declines as users quietly revert to the old way. This happens when the old process is still accessible, edge cases aren't addressed, or no one is actively monitoring and intervening. The fix requires three things: retire the old process completely (no fallback), track adoption weekly and investigate any decline, and have a standing intervention plan (office hours, champion outreach, sponsor messaging) ready to deploy when adoption stalls.
1
Get early users using the MVP for real work — not testing in a sandbox, but using it for actual tasks with real consequences.
2
Build explainability into the solution — confidence scores, reasoning, and clear guidance on when human judgment is required.
3
Deliver training and retire the old process — on launch day, the old way is no longer available. No fallback option.
4
Transition ownership to the domain — identify a domain owner, train them on prompts and content, give them an adoption playbook.
5
Monitor adoption weekly and intervene at plateaus — office hours, champion outreach, edge-case examples, sponsor messaging.
Critical

Adoption Monitoring & Intervention Playbook

Adoption doesn't sustain itself. It requires active monitoring and immediate intervention when usage plateaus or declines. Track the right metrics weekly — not just "how many people logged in," but "how many people are using it for real work instead of the old process." When adoption stalls, you need a playbook of interventions ready to deploy.

MCC Lesseps InsightAdoption Monitoring Approach

MCC tracked adoption through multiple signals: UAT participation/completion, quality/specificity of feedback, clarity of understanding among stakeholders, and ability to articulate what worked, what didn't, and what remains unknown. They framed overrides as learning signals, not failures, and used feedback loops to continuously improve the system.

Key Lesson: The mindset shifted from "Is the AI right?" to "How do we make the system better?" This learning orientation prevented the common trap of treating adoption plateaus as user resistance rather than opportunities for improvement.

Post-Launch

Sustainment Checklist — Prevent Regression

Post-launch success depends on sustaining specific behaviors that prevent regression to old ways of working. These aren't optional — they're the difference between a successful transformation and a failed deployment that gets quietly abandoned six months later.

✓
Human-based decision making remains default — AI outputs treated as decision support, not decisions. Humans explicitly review, contextualize, and approve. Overrides viewed as learning signals.
✓
Orchestration over execution becomes expected — Teams consistently orchestrate workflows rather than manually execute tasks. Work coordinated through defined processes, not personal follow-ups.
✓
Criteria-based selection replaces informal choices — Decisions rely on transparent, documented criteria. Personal networks may inform judgment but don't override the process.
✓
Standard work is followed and improved — Teams use documented "best-known" process as default. Deviations are intentional and surfaced. Improvements captured and folded back in.
✓
Learning signals actively used — Feedback, overrides, and friction points routinely reviewed. Insights inform process updates, not one-off fixes. Continuous improvement mindset.
✓
Leaders reinforce through actions — Leaders model AI-supported workflows in own decisions. Reinforce when to trust vs when judgment required. Discourage regression during pressure moments.
✓
Change artifacts remain living assets — Process documentation, guardrails kept current. CM artifacts actively used for onboarding, coaching, scaling. Ownership for maintaining assets is explicit.
⚠ Critical Success Factor
Behavioral change outlasts technical deployment
The technology might work perfectly, but if these behaviors aren't sustained, users will revert to old habits the moment they hit friction. The MCC Lesseps project explicitly defined these post-MVP continuity behaviors and made them part of the ownership transition. Without them, adoption decays within months.
Case StudyState University — Sustaining adoption through intervention

Three weeks after launching the document verification AI, adoption hit 90% — then plateaued at 85% for two weeks. The change owner investigated and found that 15% of counselors were still using the old manual checklist for "complex cases." The team ran targeted office hours showing how to handle those cases with the AI, had champion counselors reach out to holdouts, and the VP sent a message reinforcing that the old checklist was retired.

Adoption climbed back to 92% within a week and held there. The key was active monitoring and immediate intervention — not waiting for the problem to resolve itself.
