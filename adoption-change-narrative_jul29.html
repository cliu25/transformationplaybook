How-to Deep Dive

Adoption & Change

Deploying technology is the easy part. Getting people to change how they work is where most AI transformations stall. This chapter follows the same six-step transformation process used across every domain chapter (Engage through Execute) and shows specifically what adoption and change work happens at each step, not a separate track that runs alongside it, but the people-and-process work embedded inside the same steps you're already running.

Change management is not a separate workstream that runs after the build. It runs throughout the project, and what it looks like changes at each stage. In Engage, it is mapping who is affected and getting leaders aligned. In Discovery, it is involving real end users in design and starting communications. In Execute, it is running training before launch, not after, using UAT (User Acceptance Testing) as an enablement activity, and monitoring adoption closely enough to step in before a plateau becomes a loss.

Transformation is three things, not one

Every AI workflow transformation involves technology, people, and process. Most projects spend 90% of their energy on technology, forget that process needs to be re-envisioned alongside it, and treat people as an afterthought to be trained at the end. The projects that fail this way are not usually technical failures. Gartner found that half of enterprise AI project failures are change management failures, not technical ones. The model worked. The people did not adopt it.

⚙
Technology
The AI solution, the integrations, the infrastructure. This is what the build team delivers. It is necessary but not sufficient.
👥
People
The humans whose daily work changes. Their awareness, willingness, skill, and habit are what determine whether the technology actually gets used.
↻
Process
The end-to-end workflow re-envisioned around what AI makes possible. Not just bolting AI onto the existing process, but rethinking how the work gets done.

The domain owns its own change management. The AIFT team provides the framework and the support, but this guide is written for after that support steps back: the people leading the domain know their colleagues, know the culture, and know the specific risks and concerns that will drive resistance. That local knowledge is the most important input to a change plan.

Who to involve

Change management that starts at launch is too late. By the time the solution is built, the decisions that most affect user experience (what the new workflow looks like, who does what differently, what tasks disappear) have already been made without user input. Starting in Engage means those decisions are informed by the people who will live with them.

What to do in Engage for Adoption & Change
1
Name the change owner. This person is responsible for the people-and-process side of the transformation. They are not the domain transformation lead and not the product owner. They own the change plan, the communications, and the adoption tracking. On well-resourced projects this is a dedicated organizational change management (OCM) role; more often, no dedicated OCM team is assigned, and the project team absorbs these responsibilities. Either way, name a specific person, don't leave it as everyone's job, which means no one's.
2
Get the executive sponsor to communicate the vision, from the very start. Why this change is happening, what it means for the team, and what they expect from people going forward.
3
Start the communications plan now, not in Discovery. Even a rough outline of what gets said, when, and by whom keeps the message consistent from day one. The full plan gets built out once design starts, but the thinking, and the consistency it protects, starts here.
4
Re-envision the future-state workflow with input from frontline users. Document what people will do differently, not just what the AI will do. Document it clearly: this becomes the basis for UAT test cases and training materials later.
5
Map affected personas. Who does this workflow today, what will change for each, and what concerns they are likely to have. Include frontline users, managers, subject-matter experts, and adjacent teams.
6
Name at least two champions. Respected peers (not managers) who will influence adoption from within the team. Champions need early access to the solution and a specific role: they give feedback during design and carry the message to colleagues during rollout.
7
Identify the UAT lead now, not at the end. UAT (User Acceptance Testing) and change management overlap heavily, this person needs context on the program from the start so they're ready when testing begins, not brought in cold.
Step 1

Change impact assessment: understand who is affected and how

The first change management activity is a high-level read of who is affected. This is not a detailed analysis. It is enough to surface the main personas whose roles will change, and a rough sense of how those people are likely to react. That picture shapes every decision that follows: what gets built, how communications are timed, where training is invested.

For each affected persona, ask three questions: What specifically changes in their day-to-day work? What do they lose (autonomy, familiar steps, perceived expertise)? And what concerns might they have (being replaced, being evaluated differently, having to learn something new)? That last question is the most important and the one most teams skip.

PersonaWhat changesWhat they might loseLikely reaction
Frontline user Core task is now AI-assisted or automated Mastery of a familiar process; sense of contribution Skepticism, avoidance, or active workaround if not brought in early
Manager / team lead How they supervise and what they measure changes Visibility into work; familiar management rhythms Neutral to positive if outcomes improve; resistant if they lose oversight
Subject-matter expert Their specialized knowledge is now encoded in the model Status as the person who knows; request volume Often the most resistant. Or, if brought in as a champion, the most influential
Adjacent team Inputs or outputs they depend on change format or timing Predictable handoffs; established communication patterns Confused or frustrated if not notified early; easy to bring on board if informed before they surface
⚠ Pain point
Not addressing the job security question
Across IBM domains, the single most consistent source of adoption resistance is the concern that the AI is going to replace the person using it. If that question is not named and answered directly, it will run as an undercurrent through everything else. People will avoid the tool, use it out of obligation, or look for ways it fails. The answer does not need to be complex: tell people what the AI handles, what it does not handle, and what their role looks like going forward. Be specific. Vague reassurances only deepen the concern.
Step 2

Process re-envisioning: redesign the workflow, not just the technology

The most common structural failure in AI workflow transformation is designing the AI solution and then asking "what will people do with this?" That question needs to come first, not last. In Analyze (Step 2), the goal is not just to map the current process. It is to re-envision the end-to-end future-state workflow: the specific steps people will take, what AI handles, what humans handle, and what the handoffs between them look like.

This is different from the technical current/future state diagram used in Systems Integration. That one traces data flows. This one traces human activity: who does what, when, and how their judgment fits into a workflow that is partly automated. The people who do the current work are the best source of input for this, not the managers who supervise it.

⚠ Pain point
Late scramble: "what will they actually do with this?"
One of the most consistent gaps identified across domain interviews is insufficient upfront re-envisioning of the future-state process. Teams design the technology in detail and the new workflow only only vaguely. By the time the solution is close to finished, no one has a clear answer to "what will the analyst actually do different on Monday morning?" That question surfaces late, creates scope changes, and delays launch. The future-state workflow should be documented in Analyze, not discovered in UAT.

Get leaders on record in Engage. Leadership alignment is not a one-time announcement. It is a sustained behavior. But it needs to start here, because the most powerful signal users receive about whether a change is real is whether their manager takes it as a real priority. Get the executive sponsor on record with a clear statement of why this transformation is happening, what it means for the team, and what they expect from people. That statement is the foundation all later communications build on.

Case StudyMCC Lesseps: framing the change and building trust from day one

The MCC Lesseps team framed the case for change in concrete workflow terms: manual, fragmented pre-event work was creating bottlenecks that constrained speed and consistency. Not "we need to adopt AI," but a specific problem the team already felt. Leaders then modeled the AI-supported workflow in their own work rather than delegating the change downward, and the AI itself was positioned as "a fast collaborator, not an authority." That framing set the trust expectation early: the tool proposes, people decide. Users came into UAT expecting to exercise judgment, not to be replaced by the output.

Discover Adoption & Change Steps 3 & 4 · Workflow Analysis · Solution Design

Involve real users in design. Plan communications and training before the build starts.

📌 Three decisions before the build starts: who tests it (real end users, 5 to 8 of them, not managers), how you communicate at each stage, and how people learn it, including what to do when the AI is wrong.
Who to involve
Change owner Business owner Representative end users Domain transformation lead Champions Communications lead (if available)

Discovery is where the change management plan gets specific. You have a picture of who is affected from Engage. Now you use that picture to make three decisions: who participates in UAT, what the communications plan looks like across the whole project, and how training will be delivered. All three need to be decided before the build starts, not scrambled together at launch.

What to design in Discovery for Adoption & Change
1
Recruit UAT participants. Frontline workers who do the actual work, not managers. Aim for 5 to 8 people who represent the range of users: different experience levels, different use patterns, different levels of enthusiasm about change.
2
Write the communications plan. For each phase (pre-build, pre-launch, launch, post-launch), name the message, the audience, the channel, and who sends it. The executive sponsor sends the first message. A manager or champion sends the training reminder. Peer success stories come after launch.
3
Define the "what is in it for me" message per persona. One message for frontline users (time back, less drudgery, more interesting work). A different message for managers (better visibility, faster outcomes). A different message for subject-matter experts (their knowledge preserved and scaled).
4
Design the training approach before the build starts. Decide: is this workflow-embedded (a job aid on the screen while they work), a short guided walkthrough before first use, or a peer demo from a champion? Training should cover the happy path, what to do when the AI is wrong, and where to get help.
5
Build a feedback channel into the solution itself, not as a separate survey. Users who encounter problems need an immediate way to flag them. That feedback drives the Sprint 2 and 3 improvements that turn an imperfect MVP into something people want to use.
Step 3

Recruit real UAT users before design is finished

UAT (User Acceptance Testing) is the single best adoption lever available during the build. The people who test the solution before launch become its first advocates after launch, if you involve the right people. The right people are frontline workers who do the actual work, not the managers who supervise it. A manager who approves the solution and a frontline worker who uses it daily have different views of what "working" means.

Recruit UAT participants in Discovery, not the week before testing starts. Give them enough context about what the solution is designed to do that their feedback is informed. When they find problems (and they will) close the feedback loop fast. An open feedback loop that users can see builds more trust than a polished demo.

⚠ Pain point
Running UAT with managers instead of end users
The most consistent UAT mistake is recruiting the people who are easy to schedule (managers, leads, and power users) instead of the people who will use the tool every day. These groups have different workflows, different concerns, and different definitions of success. A manager who sees a 40% efficiency improvement in aggregate may approve the solution with enthusiasm. A frontline worker who now has to handle the 5% of cases the AI gets wrong, without any guidance on how to do that, will avoid the tool. Test with the people who will live with the solution.
Step 4

Plan communications: right message, right time, right channel

Communications for an AI transformation are not a launch announcement. They are a sustained conversation that starts in Engage and continues after the solution goes live. The message changes at each stage, and what people need to hear before they have seen the tool is different from what they need after they have used it once.

Engage / Discovery
Why this is happening
What problem we are solving. Why now. What role you will have in shaping it.

Channel: Team meeting, executive message
Build (before launch)
What is changing and what it means for you
Specific changes to the workflow. What you will do differently. What AI handles. What your role still owns.

Channel: Team briefing, job aid, FAQ
Launch and after
How to use it and where to get help
Hands-on guidance, edge case handling, feedback channel. Regular updates on how adoption is going.

Channel: Training, office hours, Slack, manager reinforcement

The most important communication is the "what is in it for me" message for each persona. This is not the same for everyone. A user who spends 60% of their time on manual document checks wants to hear that they will spend that time on higher-value work instead. A subject-matter expert wants to hear that their knowledge is being preserved, not replaced. Tailor the core message to the specific gain each group gets from the change.

⚠ Pain point
Announcing the change once and assuming people heard it
A single launch email does not change behavior. People need to encounter the message multiple times, in multiple formats, from multiple voices: their manager, a peer champion, a brief in a team meeting, a follow-up from the executive sponsor. And the message needs to be specific: "we are implementing an AI tool" is less useful than "starting Monday, when an order fails validation, the system will flag it automatically and send you the exception with a reason code. Here is what you do from there." Specific, action-oriented communications land. Abstract ones do not.

Consistency matters as much as repetition. If the details shift between messages, how many agents, what exactly gets automated, when it launches, that inconsistency reads as uncertainty even when no one names it, and it erodes the trust every other communication is trying to build. Say the same specific thing every time until it changes, and when it changes, say why. Just as important: be explicit about what the change will not do, not just what it will. Negative reactions after launch are usually a symptom of unmet expectations, someone was told the tool would do more than it does, not a reaction to the change itself. Stating both sides plainly up front, this is what ships, this is what does not, prevents that gap from opening in the first place.

Step 4 continued

Design training before the build starts

Training that happens after launch misses the window when people are most open to it. The best moment to train someone on a new tool is before they use it for the first time, with enough guidance to feel competent from the first interaction. Training designed after the solution is built is often rushed, covers the happy path, and misses the edge cases users will encounter in their first week.

Training for AI tools is not the same as training for software. People need to understand not just how to use the tool but when to trust it, when to check its output, and what to do when it is wrong. That last point (how to handle AI errors) is the training content most often missing from rollouts, and it is the gap that drives avoidance. If users do not know what to do when the AI gets it wrong, they stop using it for anything where it might get it wrong.

The future-state workflow you documented back in Engage is not a one-time artifact. Write it down clearly there and it becomes the foundation for two things later: the test cases your UAT lead uses to actually validate the solution, and the training materials that teach people how to do the job. Document it vaguely, and both UAT and training end up reconstructing that same knowledge from scratch, later, under more time pressure.

Case StudyMCC Lesseps: tailored messaging and learning in the flow of work

The MCC Lesseps team ran persona-specific engagement rather than one broadcast: event owners, managers, and leadership each got messaging tailored to what would change for them. Training was embedded in the workflow itself (short guidance and examples at the point of use) instead of formal training courses, so people learned the tool while doing real work. And UAT was run on real events, not sandbox scenarios, which meant testers built competence on the actual work they would be doing after launch.

Step 5

Experimentation: start small, drive real usage, treat resistance as data

The most powerful adoption technique is the one that sounds the most obvious: have real people use the actual tool for real work, starting small. Not a demo. Not a sandbox. A real task, with a real outcome, inside the actual workflow. What happens at that first moment of real use determines whether the person becomes an advocate or an avoider.

How to run the rollout and sustain adoption
1
Choose your rollout approach. Two options: phased (one team or location first, expand after two to four weeks of stable adoption) or all at once (full user group on day one, more coordination required but faster to scale). Phased rollouts give you a contained feedback loop before full exposure. All-at-once works when training is solid, the change is low-risk, and the user group is small.
2
Complete training before launch day, not on it. Workflow-embedded training (a job aid visible in the tool, a 15-minute peer walkthrough) is more effective than a group session the week after launch.
3
Retire the old process explicitly on launch day. Communicate what is being turned off, when, and what to do if a case requires a different approach. Vague exceptions become general exceptions.
4
Track adoption metrics weekly for the first eight weeks. Assign the change owner to review the numbers every week and flag any plateau or drop within 48 hours.
5
Respond to resistance with investigation, not pressure. When a user is not adopting, find out why before deciding what to do. A champion conversation works better than a manager mandate for most resistance patterns.
6
Close the feedback loop visibly. When users flag a problem and see it fixed in the next sprint, they trust the channel. When they flag problems and hear nothing, they stop flagging and stop using the tool.
7
Celebrate the first real wins with the team that created them. A user who reduced verification time from 14 hours to 2 hours is a more powerful advocate than any communication from leadership. Share that story, with specifics.

Start with a narrow scope and willing participants. The goal is to build a proof point that a real person can point to: "I did this task with the tool, and here is what happened." One concrete result from a real colleague is worth more than a hundred slides. Once that proof point exists, expand. A hesitant manager who sees a specific colleague succeed is far more likely to try it themselves than one who has only heard an abstract case.

Case StudyR2A Finance: start small to drive the mindset shift

The R2A team had a finance manager who was skeptical about the AI analysis tool. Rather than arguing the case or waiting until the solution was fully polished, the team asked him to pick just one of the seven analysis areas his team handled and use the tool for real work in that area only. No other tools were allowed for that analysis. The constraint was deliberate: it forced real usage rather than hedged comparison.

The next day, the manager came back and asked to run the tool across four areas that month, and all seven the month after. He went from resistant to advocate in 24 hours, not because the tool was perfect (it was not) but because he did real work with it and saw a real result. Gradual, constrained, real adoption beats waiting for perfection.

Resistance is data, not a problem to overcome. When users avoid the tool, use it without enthusiasm, or actively work around it, those behaviors are signals about what is not working: the training, the edge case handling, the workflow design, or the underlying concern about what the change means for their role. Investigate before intervening. A user who avoids the tool because they do not know how to handle exceptions needs different support than a user who avoids it because they do not trust the output.

Signal: avoidance
Trust gap
User does not trust the AI output enough to act on it. Response: add explainability to the output (why did it produce this result?), share accuracy data, let champions demo real results.
Signal: workaround
Workflow friction
The new process is harder than the old one, sometimes for one specific case, sometimes across the board because of a design or execution gap. Response: investigate whether it's isolated or systemic, then fix the friction in the next sprint or document the exception handling path clearly.
Signal: minimal use
Training gap
User knows the tool exists but does not feel confident enough to use it for real work. Response: targeted one-on-one walkthrough from a champion, not another group training session.
Signal: vocal pushback
Concern not addressed
User has an unaddressed concern about what the change means for their role or their standing. Response: a direct conversation. What specifically do you think will change? Answer it directly.

The manager's role during Experimentation is not to monitor compliance. It is to visibly use the tool themselves and reinforce the new workflow in how they talk about the work. Prosci research shows that projects with active, visible executive and manager sponsorship are more likely to succeed. If the manager keeps referring to metrics from the old process, the team will keep working the old way.

Step 6

Scale & Adopt: launch with training done, monitor weekly, retire the old way

By the time the solution is ready to launch to the full user group, three things need to already be in place: training completed (not scheduled), the old process retired (not just "no longer required"), and the adoption metrics defined and tracked. Launching without training is the most consistent cause of adoption plateau. Leaving the old process available is the most consistent cause of reversion.

Training before launch, not after. The window when people are most open to learning is before they use the tool for the first time in a real context. Training delivered after launch competes with the pressure of live work and is attended by people who are already forming habits (good or bad) about the new tool. Training should happen in the week before launch, be specific to the actual workflow, and include what to do when the AI is wrong.

Retire the old process explicitly. As long as the old way of doing the work remains available, there will be a subset of users who use it, for "complex cases," for "times when the AI isn't quite right," or out of habit. Retiring the old process is not about forcing compliance. It is about removing the path of least resistance back to the status quo. If the old process needs to stay available for some cases, document exactly which cases and what triggers them. Vague exceptions become general exceptions.

⚠ Pain point
Declaring victory at launch
The most common adoption failure is treating launch as the end of the change effort. Adoption at the end of week one is a lagging indicator of training quality, not a leading indicator of sustained use. Real adoption takes six to eight weeks to establish as habit. The teams that sustain high adoption are the ones that track usage weekly, investigate every plateau right away, and stay close to users for the full post-launch period, not just the first two weeks.

Adoption metrics: what to track and when

Track adoption metrics weekly during the first eight weeks after launch. The metrics that matter are not the same as the value metrics from the measurement chapter. These are behavioral signals that tell you whether the change is taking hold.

MetricWhat it tells youWhen to act
Active usage rate
% of eligible users who used the tool this week		 Whether people are actually using the tool, not just trained on it Below 60% after week 2: investigate why with a direct conversation, not a survey
Repeat usage rate
% of week 1 users still using in week 4		 Whether usage is becoming habit or a one-time trial Drop greater than 15% from week 1 to week 4: identify what is driving the drop-off
Workaround rate
% of eligible tasks still done the old way		 Whether the old process has been retired Any workaround above 10%: identify the specific cases driving it and address them explicitly
User satisfaction
Brief pulse: would you recommend this tool to a colleague?		 Leading indicator of sustained adoption and advocacy Negative trend two weeks running: run listening sessions to understand the specific friction
Feedback volume
Issues flagged through the in-tool feedback channel		 Whether users trust the feedback channel and whether you are closing the loop Sudden drop in feedback often means users stopped expecting a response. Close the loop visibly.
Case StudyMCC Speaker Agent: catching the adoption plateau before it became a loss

After the Speaker Agent launched, the MCC team tracked active usage weekly. Week 1 opened at 65%, climbed to 85% by week 2, and reached 90% by week 3. Then it dropped to 85% in weeks 4 and 5. Without weekly tracking, that drop would not have been noticed for a month. With it, the change owner investigated within days and found that 15% of users were still using the old manual checklist for "complex cases."

The team ran targeted office hours showing how to handle those specific cases with the AI, had champion users reach out to holdouts directly, and the executive sponsor sent a message confirming that the old checklist was retired. Adoption climbed back to 92% within a week and held there. The active monitoring and fast intervention, not the launch itself, is what produced sustained adoption.

Case StudyMCC Lesseps: monitoring beyond usage and sustaining the change

The MCC Lesseps team tracked more than logins. They watched the quality and specificity of user feedback, how clearly stakeholders understood the system, and whether users could articulate what worked, what did not, and what was still unknown. Overrides were framed as learning signals, not failures: each one was a data point about where the system needed improvement. The question the team asked shifted from "Is the AI right?" to "How do we make the system better?" That reframe prevented the common trap of reading an adoption plateau as user resistance when it is actually a signal about the product.

Post-launch, the team sustained specific behaviors rather than declaring victory at deployment: AI output treated as decision support with human review as the default, work coordinated through the defined process instead of personal follow-ups, decisions made on documented criteria, and leaders modeling the new workflow and holding the line when pressure tempted people back to the old way.
