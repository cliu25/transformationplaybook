Value Measurement & ROI

Good measurement is not about reporting up. It is about knowing whether what you built is working, having the evidence to prove it, and using that evidence to make the next decision. This chapter helps you define the right metrics for your workflow, track them in a way that is honest and lightweight, and build the proof needed to sustain and grow what you have built.

This chapter follows the same six-step transformation process used across every domain chapter (Engage through Execute) and shows what value measurement and ROI work happens at each step.

Active across Engage → Discover → Execute

Why measurement matters

📌 Metrics exist for your domain, not for the program. Pick the 3 to 5 workflow metrics you control and can defend. Organization-wide AI metrics are tracked by AIFT and IBM executive leaders and are not your job.

Across IBM domains, the most consistent friction around metrics is the concern that tracking will be used to judge rather than help. The reality is the opposite. The teams that skip measurement are the ones who cannot make the case to keep going, cannot get resources for Wave 2, and cannot prove to leadership that the investment was worth it. Good metrics exist for the domain, not for the program.

Q2C example: why tracking your own metrics pays off

Teams that define and track their own metrics have a structural advantage when it comes to defending their work, securing continued investment, and knowing when to pivot. For example, the Q2C (Quote-to-Cash) team tracks Effort Reduction and Cycle Time sprint over sprint not because they have to. When the data showed effort reduction climbing from 8% in Sprint 1 to 37% by Sprint 5, that story sold the next phase of the program. Measurement is the evidence you are building.

Two types of metrics exist here. Workflow metrics are the ones you define, track, and own: effort reduction, cycle time, cost per transaction, quality scores, adoption of your specific tool. Organization-wide metrics like overall AI usage rates or AI literacy completion are tracked by AIFT and IBM executive leaders. This chapter focuses on the metrics that serve your team.

Productivity
Time per task, steps removed, effort reduction %, hours saved per person per week on this workflow
Quality
Error rate, false pass/fail rate, rework volume, output accuracy, exception rate
Cycle time
End-to-end workflow duration, step-to-step latency, processing speed, time to completion
Adoption
% of eligible users active on your specific tool, frequency of use, retention after launch
Financial
Cost per transaction, vendor spend replaced, headcount redeployment, ROI against development cost
Satisfaction
NPS (Net Promoter Score) with your tool, OSAT (Overall Satisfaction score), user-reported time saved, qualitative feedback on this workflow

The most important thing to know is that you are not limited to a predefined list. The right metrics are the ones that tell a clear story about whether your specific workflow improved. For example, the Q2C team measures False Pass Rate and False Fail Rate because those tell them whether the AI validation agent is accurate. That metric matters for their workflow and would be irrelevant for most others. Pick the 3 to 5 metrics that would make a skeptic believe the transformation worked.

Engage Value Measurement & ROI Steps 1 & 2 · Tech & Data Assessment · Business Process Mapping

Agree on what success looks like before you build anything.

📌 One focused metrics design session, before any solution is designed, prevents the most common failure: defining metrics after the build to make the results look good.
Who to involve
Domain transformation lead Process SMEs Leadership or EBM contact Executive sponsor

The single most valuable measurement activity you can do happens before any solution is designed: a short metrics design session with your process SMEs and business leadership. A focused conversation — not a workshop — to agree on three things: what success looks like, what the current process costs to run, and what a meaningful improvement would be. The output is a shared definition of success before a line of requirements is written.

Step 1

Tech & Data Assessment: agree on what to measure and what good looks like

3 to 5 people: the domain transformation lead, 1 to 2 process SMEs, and business leadership or your EBM contact. Walk away with a shared answer to each of the following:

What to agree on in the metrics design session
1
What is the primary value this transformation delivers? Identify the main category: productivity (time and effort), quality (accuracy and errors), cycle time (speed), or cost (spend). This shapes which metrics matter most. Most workflows have one primary and at most one secondary.
2
What does the workflow cost to run today? Get specific with your process SMEs: how long does step A to step B take? How many people touch it? What is the error or rework rate? What is the cost per transaction? These are your baselines. Actual numbers from the platform are better than estimates.
3
What would a meaningful improvement look like? Set a direction before the solution is designed, not after. "We expect 40% effort reduction in this step" is a hypothesis that shapes the build. Get business leadership, or your EBM contact where one exists, to sense-check whether the numbers make sense and whether they tie to something on the balance sheet.
4
How will you capture the data? Platform telemetry is the best option. If your tool already tracks time-in-step, use it. Manual tracking (time logs, simple spreadsheets) is acceptable. Surveys are a weak fallback because they measure perception, not performance. The data source you choose now is the one you will be held to later.
5
Who owns each metric? Name a person for each one. Metrics without owners do not get tracked.

Frame the financial value in the terms leadership uses. Three habits do most of the work:

  • Classify financial impact as revenue contribution, cost savings, or risk avoidance. Every financial metric you define should map to one of them.
  • Keep a projected figure and a realized figure, separately labeled. Leadership treats these two numbers very differently, and blending them is how measurement credibility is lost.
  • Anchor targets against reference points that hold up. Two useful markers: around two hours saved per person per week, and 60%+ of intended users adopting the tool within six months of launch.

The best baseline data comes from inside the platform people already use. Building your measurement approach around instrumentation that already exists is more reliable than setting up new tracking after the fact.

Step 2

Business Process Mapping: document what the workflow costs today

Each metric needs all five columns filled before the build starts. The Reasoning column is the most important: if you cannot state clearly why this metric matters for this workflow, it is not the right metric. Use the same source for baseline and post-launch measurement so the comparison holds.
⚠ Pain point
Skipping the metrics conversation because it feels premature
Most teams put off the metrics conversation because the solution does not exist yet and it feels speculative. The result is that they arrive at the end of the build with no agreed baseline, no defined target, and a team that starts negotiating what success means after the fact. The metrics design session does not require a complete solution. It requires a clear enough understanding of the workflow to say what "better" looks like. That exists at Engage.
Case StudyMCC Event Speaker Agent: defining metrics before building

The MCC team ran their metrics conversation during Workflow Analysis before any design work started. They measured the current manual process with their event planning SMEs: average time to identify and confirm speakers was 5 days, the average number of back-and-forth emails per event was 12, and the speaker confirmation rate was 65%. They set targets before the build: 2 days, 3 emails, 85% confirmation rate. The EBM team confirmed the metrics tied to G&A reduction through event planning efficiency. They tracked weekly using a spreadsheet linked to their event management system. When the solution launched, the baseline was already there. No scrambling to reconstruct what things looked like before.

Discover Value Measurement & ROI Steps 3 & 4 · Workflow Analysis · Solution Design
Step 3

Workflow Analysis

Lock the metrics. Build them into the solution from day one.

📌 Capture baselines from the platform before anything changes, and write tracking into the requirements. Retrofitted measurement is unreliable and sometimes impossible.
Who to involve
Domain transformation lead Leadership or EBM contact Product owner CIO/BPT platform team (for instrumentation)

Discovery is where the candidate metrics from Engage get confirmed against real data and locked in. Workflow Analysis gives you the opportunity to go deeper on the current baseline, capturing actual numbers from source systems rather than estimates. Solution Design is where you make instrumentation a requirement in the MVP, so the data is collected automatically from Sprint 1 (the first two-week development cycle), not retrofitted after launch.

Step 3

Workflow Analysis: confirm baselines from source data

Use this time to capture baselines from platform data. For each metric: what is the actual current value, where does it come from, and can we capture it the same way after launch? The before-and-after comparison is only credible if both halves come from the same source.

The most defensible measurement is a before-and-after comparison on the same metric from the same source. If you track step-time in the current system, track step-time in the AI-enabled system. That is the evidence that survives scrutiny.
⚠ Pain point
The baseline does not exist when the build ends
The most consistent tracking failure is launching a solution, seeing improvement, and not being able to prove how much because no one captured what things looked like before. Sometimes the old tool is decommissioned; sometimes the process has already changed. Capture the baseline in Workflow Analysis, before anything changes. One hour of data collection now saves months of reconstruction later — and often the reconstruction is not possible at all.
Step 4

Solution Design: make tracking a requirement, not an afterthought

For each metric from Engage and Workflow Analysis, the requirements specify what the solution must capture, how it captures it, and where that data goes. If the product owner and platform team cannot tell you how Hours Saved will be measured in the MVP, that is a gap in the requirements. Work with the CIO/BPT platform team to confirm what is possible. If EBM integration (such as Apptio) is planned, that data pipeline needs to be scoped in design, not wired up after launch.

Where EBM fits. For CIO-driven projects (via Path to Production / OnboardAI), EBM is partnering with the CIO AI Platform team on value measurement upfront. For domain and business-unit-led AI projects, EBM is introducing a tiered measurement model, from lightweight EIW mapping for smaller initiatives to full cost-for-performance tracking for flagship investments, so every AI use case is measured at the right level of rigor. Stay tuned for more.

⚠ Pain point
Building the solution and then trying to add tracking
Instrumentation is far harder to add after the solution is built than to include from the start. A solution that did not collect usage data from Sprint 1 has a gap in its pilot evidence. A solution that does not log time-in-step has to rely on surveys to estimate productivity impact. Neither is as credible as data captured automatically throughout the build. Make telemetry a requirement in the MVP. If it cannot be built in, decide now whether you are comfortable with the measurement approach you will be left with.
💡 No dedicated technical support?
This checklist assumes someone technical is at the table, event triggers, data pipelines, and platform telemetry are not business-user work. If you don't have a technical partner assigned yet, that's your actual first step, not this checklist. Systems Integration's resourcing guidance covers how to get one: some domains partner with the CIO organization for build resources; if yours does, your domain transformation lead works with them on prioritization. If resources come from elsewhere, bring that group into planning the same way, before you're stuck answering questions like these alone.
What to specify in Solution Design for each metric
1
What event triggers a data point? User completes a step, AI returns a result, a transaction closes. Be specific about what gets recorded and when.
2
Where is the data stored? The platform's native logs, a custom table, EPM, or Apptio (the common front end for EBM value tracking). For EBM, the preferred path is data in EPM, which EBM can then read; csv or xls works for a POC and initial steps, but the long-term direction is an automated pipeline to EPM.
3
Who on the build team owns the instrumentation? Assign it like any other requirement. Instrumentation without an owner does not get built.
4
When will you validate the data pipeline? Sprint 1 is the target. If the telemetry is not working by the end of Sprint 1, you are not capturing the pilot evidence you need.
5
If MVP scope changes, update the metrics. A metric tied to a feature that was de-scoped is a metric you cannot report. Keep the metric list aligned with what is being built.

Case StudyR2A (Reva) Order Validation: tracking per sprint from day one

The R2A team built measurement into the solution design for every sprint. The Apptio decision dashboard was scoped as part of the build, not added later. Sprint by sprint, effort reduction grew: 8.69% in Sprint 1, 7.65% in Sprint 2, 16.39% in Sprint 3, 22.88% in Sprint 4, 37.77% in Sprint 5. That trajectory was only possible because the tracking was in place from the first sprint.

Ready to move to Execute

Baselines are confirmed from source data. Metrics are locked. Instrumentation is a named requirement in the MVP scope with an owner and a Sprint 1 validation date. Business leadership has seen and endorsed the business case. You are building with measurement built in.

Execute Value Measurement & ROI Steps 5 & 6 · Experimentation · Scale & Adopt
Step 5

Experimentation

Track sprint over sprint. Use the data to make the go/no-go decision and what comes next.

📌 Measured actuals from real work, not projections, are what make the go/no-go credible. A flat metric in Sprint 2 is information to act on, not a failure to hide.
Who to involve
Domain transformation lead Product owner Leadership or EBM contact Business owner (after the pilot) Executive sponsor
Step 5

Experimentation: track weekly, report at steering, use data to pivot

Track every two weeks, aligned to sprint cycles. Bring the numbers to steering as the basis for decisions, not a status update. If Effort Reduction is flat at 8% after two sprints and the target is 40%, that is information — the approach, feature set, or target needs adjustment. Catching that in Sprint 2 is very different from catching it at the end of the pilot.

Measure from real work. That evidence ("this step took 45 minutes before and takes 8 minutes now") is what makes the go/no-go credible. Projections do not survive leadership scrutiny the same way measured actuals do.

Example: Q2C effort reduction sprint over sprint. The trajectory across five sprints (8% to 38%) made the case for the next phase of the program. This story only exists because the tracking was in place from Sprint 1.
⚠ Pain point
Only measuring at the end of the pilot instead of sprint over sprint
Teams that only look at metrics at the end-of-pilot go/no-go meeting miss the most valuable use of data during the build: course correction. If effort reduction is not improving sprint over sprint, something in the approach needs to change. The earlier you see that, the more time you have to address it. Weekly or bi-weekly tracking during the build is not administrative overhead. It is the mechanism that keeps the build focused on the outcome rather than on feature delivery.
Step 5

The end-of-pilot go/no-go decision (Day 90 for a standard AIFT sprint)

The end-of-pilot decision is a business decision. Bring the actual metrics versus the baseline and target agreed in Engage. Report projected value and realized value separately — never blended. A go decision does not mean all metrics hit target; it means the trajectory is credible and Wave 2 is justified. A no-go is the measurement working as intended.

Step 6

Scale & Adopt: sustain tracking after launch and use it to grow

Tracking shifts from sprint cadence to monthly reporting. The business owner takes over the reporting tracker; business leadership validates realized value. The data is what you use to justify the Wave 2 roadmap, secure capacity, and tell the story of what the transformation accomplished. Teams that stop tracking after the go decision find that executive support drifts because there is no ongoing evidence of impact.

Keep your AI System record current. If built through Path to Production, update the AI System record as realized value comes in — it keeps your solution visible to leadership without re-presenting it.

How to sustain value tracking after launch
1
Hand off the KPI dashboard or equivalent reporting tracker to the business owner before the build team steps back (a shared spreadsheet works if a dashboard tool is not available). Monthly reporting on the same metrics tracked during the pilot. No new framework, no new system.
2
Validate hard dollar savings with business leadership quarterly. Not estimated: confirmed. This is what makes the business case defensible when someone asks how the investment performed.
3
Use the data to prioritize the backlog. If effort reduction plateaued at 38% and the target is 70%, what feature in Wave 2 closes the gap? Let the metric data drive sequencing, not gut feel about what is most interesting to build.
4
Build the ROI narrative for the next phase. The data from this workflow (the trajectory, the actuals against baseline, the leadership-validated savings) is the business case for Wave 2. Document it in the ROI Analysis before the team moves on.

Case StudyMCC Speaker Agent: sustained tracking after launch

After launch, the MCC team found 85% of event planners had tried the agent but only 60% were using it regularly. They diagnosed the gap, fixed the edge cases routing planners back to the manual process, ran targeted training, and within six weeks adoption was above 80%. The six-month ROI analysis showed 2.8 days saved per event (down from 5) and speaker confirmation rate up to 81% — validated by the EBM team and used to secure resources for the next phase.
