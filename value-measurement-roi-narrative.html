Key Challenge

Value Measurement & ROI

AI-first transformation value must be defined before the solution is built and validated after the solution is adopted. This chapter helps domain leads move from a high-level value hypothesis to a measurable business case, then from pilot evidence to realized value reporting. The goal is not to create a complex finance model; the goal is to define a simple, credible, repeatable way to prove whether the transformation improved the workflow.

Active across Engage → Discover → Execute
Engage · Value Measurement & ROI Step 1 · Assess · Business Process Mapping

Start with a hypothesis. Pick what you can actually measure.

By the time you're ready to measure value, you need to answer one question first: what kind of value are you creating? The four categories from the AIFT Scorecard are Process (hard and soft KPIs), People (workforce readiness and adoption), Technology (use case penetration and cost savings), and Data (quality and governance). Each category has specific metrics that roll up to executive-level reporting. Pick the primary category for your use case — don't try to claim all four at once.

The value hypothesis at this stage is directional, not precise. You're answering: is this opportunity worth $500K or $5M? Is the baseline measurable at all, or are we guessing? Can we instrument this, or will we be stuck with surveys? These questions screen whether the opportunity is real before you invest in detailed metric design. The AIFT Scorecard provides the framework — your job is to identify which metrics apply to your specific workflow transformation.

The AIFT Scorecard organizes metrics into four categories tracked quarterly. Domain teams select relevant metrics from each category based on their workflow transformation. All metrics roll up to executive-level reporting with Finance validation.
⚠ Pain point
Building a complex metric model before the opportunity is even qualified
The most common mistake here is spending weeks building a detailed ROI model before confirming the opportunity is worth pursuing. The Engage phase needs a rough value hypothesis — a category, a directional size, and a candidate baseline source. If you can't identify where the baseline data lives or how you'd measure improvement, that's a signal to stop or pivot, not to build a more elaborate model. Save the precision for Discover, after you've confirmed the workflow is worth transforming.

Also confirm early whether you have a Finance or EBM partner who will validate your measurement approach. Without that partnership, your business case will lack credibility when it reaches executive review. The partner doesn't need to be deeply involved yet — just identified and willing to engage when you're ready to lock metrics in Discover.

1
Select relevant AIFT Scorecard metrics — identify which Process, People, Technology, and Data metrics apply to your workflow transformation.
2
Draft a rough size of the prize — directional estimate ($500K? $5M?) to screen whether it's worth pursuing. Reference scorecard targets.
3
Identify baseline data sources — where does current-state data live? System data preferred over surveys. Can it be instrumented?
4
Confirm Finance/EBM partner — who will validate measurement approach and ensure metrics align with quarterly scorecard reporting?

Discover · Value Measurement & ROI Step 2 · Workflow Analysis · Solution Design

Lock the metrics before you design anything. This is what the build optimizes toward.

Discover has two jobs for value measurement. First, during Workflow Analysis, you lock the metrics and baselines — the 3-5 leading indicators that define success, captured from source systems (not estimates). Second, during Solution Design, you bake measurement into the solution architecture so value is tracked automatically, not retrofitted.

The metrics you define here are what the entire build optimizes toward. If you wait until after the pilot to define them, you'll optimize for the wrong thing — or worse, you'll define metrics after the fact to make the results look good. The discipline is simple: before any code is written, agree on what you're measuring, what the baseline is, what the target is, and how you'll capture it.

Step 3

Workflow Analysis — Define Metrics and Baselines

The Workflow Analysis is where you move from a directional hypothesis to specific, measurable targets. You're defining 3-5 leading metrics tied to your value category, capturing the current baseline from source systems, and deciding how each metric will be measured. You're also setting the payback threshold — the bar for deciding later whether the solution is worth scaling.

The most important decision here is measurement approach. Instrumented platform tracking is preferred — it's auditable, continuous, and doesn't depend on user memory. Surveys are a weak fallback, used only when instrumentation isn't possible. If you're replacing an old tool, set up an apples-to-apples comparison of the same step in both platforms. That's the evidence that survives executive scrutiny.

⚠ Pain point
Waiting until after the pilot to define metrics
If you haven't locked the go/no-go criteria before Day 1 of the build, they'll be defined after the build — which means they'll be defined to pass. The Day 90 decision needs to answer a pre-agreed question: did this build demonstrate enough value, at enough reliability, to justify scaling? Write that question down. Agree on the data source. Get sponsor sign-off before development starts. A metric with no pre-agreed threshold is decoration, not a decision rule.
Move from current state baseline to defined metrics to target state. Choose instrumented tracking whenever possible — it's the only measurement approach that survives executive scrutiny.
1
Select 3-5 AIFT Scorecard metrics relevant to your workflow. Capture current baseline from source systems, not estimates.
2
Decide measurement approach — instrumented platform tracking preferred (e.g., telemetry, system logs); surveys only as weak fallback.
3
Set targets aligned with scorecard — reference quarterly targets (e.g., 60% adoption, 2 hrs saved/week, 40% daily usage).
4
Draft lightweight business case — tie to scorecard categories (Process cost savings, People adoption, Technology penetration, Data quality).
5
Specify for each metric: name, baseline, target, source system, owner, measurement cadence (quarterly for scorecard reporting).
6
Get Finance/EBM validation — confirm metrics align with scorecard framework and quarterly reporting requirements.
Step 4

Solution Design — Bake in Instrumentation

With metrics locked, Solution Design's job is to bake measurement into the solution architecture. This means specifying the telemetry and instrumentation the MVP must capture for each metric — auto-instrumentation built in from day one, so value tracking doesn't depend on manual collection.

If MVP scope changes during design, the metrics must be updated to match. A reporting model that doesn't align with what you're actually building is worse than no reporting model at all.

⚠ Pain point
Treating instrumentation as a post-launch add-on
The second most common failure is treating measurement as something to "add later." By the time you realize you need it, the solution is already built without the hooks to capture the data. The fix is simple: make telemetry and instrumentation a requirement in the MVP scope, not a nice-to-have. If the solution can't auto-capture the metrics you defined in Workflow Analysis, it's not ready to build.
1
Specify telemetry requirements for each metric in the MVP scope.
2
Update metrics to reflect any scope changes during design.
3
Draft reporting model — how will metrics be visualized and shared with stakeholders?
4
Confirm auto-capture mechanisms are designed into solution architecture.

Execute · Value Measurement & ROI Experimentation · Scale & Adopt

Track value throughout the build. Validate with Finance after launch. Report quarterly to scorecard.

Execute is where value measurement moves from plan to evidence. During the build, you're tracking pilot metrics against baseline weekly — not waiting until the end to check. After launch, you're validating realized value with Finance and reporting actual vs. target to the AIFT Scorecard quarterly. The discipline here is transparency: red on a chart is good, because it surfaces problems while there's still time to fix them. All metrics roll up to executive scorecard reporting.

Step 5

Experimentation — Track Value During Build

The build team's first job — before any code — is to confirm the solution is instrumented to auto-capture the metrics defined in Discover. Then, during 2-week sprint cycles, they track pilot metrics against baseline weekly. This continuous evidence informs pivot/continue/stop decisions and de-risks the scaling case.

The most important moment is the Day 90 go/no-go decision. The data either supports scaling or it doesn't. The decision should be made on the metrics defined before the build started, not on enthusiasm or organizational momentum. If the solution didn't hit the payback threshold, document why and either adjust or stop. A no-go isn't a failure — it means the measurement worked.

⚠ Pain point
Not instrumenting the solution to auto-capture metrics
The most common build-phase failure is discovering at Day 60 that the solution can't capture the metrics you need. By then, it's too late to retrofit instrumentation without delaying the launch. The fix is simple: make telemetry a Day 1 requirement, not a Day 60 discovery. If the solution can't auto-capture the metrics, it's not ready to demo, let alone launch.
Track pilot metrics weekly during 2-week sprints. The trend line tells you whether you're on track to hit the payback threshold at Day 90. After launch, metrics feed into quarterly AIFT Scorecard reporting. Red early is good — it gives you time to adjust.
1
Instrument the solution to automatically capture metrics (telemetry, usage tracking, EBM/Apptio integration).
2
Track pilot metrics weekly during 2-week sprints against baseline.
3
Adjust targets if baseline assumptions were wrong — credible targets maintain stakeholder confidence.
4
Have real users test early — track actual time/effort saved on real work, not test scenarios.
5
Document value trajectory for steering committee — transparent evidence enables informed decisions.
6
Make Day 90 go/no-go decision based on whether results hit the payback threshold.
Step 6

Scale & Adopt — Validate and Report Realized Value

After launch, the work shifts from tracking pilot evidence to validating realized value. This means comparing real results against the starting baseline, having Finance confirm those results are accurate, and reporting actual vs. target metrics quarterly to the AIFT Scorecard. All domain metrics roll up to executive-level Process, People, Technology, and Data categories.

The goal is sustained executive confidence and a reusable value story for the next domain. If adoption barriers prevent value realization, identify and address them. If the solution is working, scale successful patterns to other domains. The value tracking doesn't stop when the project team leaves — it becomes part of ongoing business operations and quarterly scorecard reporting.

⚠ Pain point
Value tracking stops after the project team leaves
The final failure mode is declaring victory at launch and stopping all value tracking. Six months later, when leadership asks "did this actually work?", no one can answer with data. The fix is ownership: before handoff, name the individual (not the role) who will monitor post-launch metrics, report them quarterly to the AIFT Scorecard, and escalate if adoption drops or value doesn't materialize. If that person isn't named and committed, the solution will drift.
Post-launch value validation flows through Finance review to confirmed ROI, then into monthly reporting across four stakeholder groups. This cadence sustains executive confidence and enables scaling decisions.
1
Monitor post-launch metrics through EBM dashboards or value tracking system.
2
Validate realized value with Finance — compare real results against baseline, get Finance confirmation.
3
Report actual vs. target monthly to Finance/EBM and executive cadences.
4
Identify and address adoption barriers that prevent value realization.
5
Scale successful patterns to other domains or use cases.
6
Continuously optimize based on usage data and user feedback.

Case StudyManufacturing Transformation — Sustained Value Tracking

After launching an AI-powered system integration solution, the Manufacturing domain tracked value realization for 6 months through their EBM dashboard. They achieved a 40% reduction in system integration time and 30% improvement in data processing efficiency, exceeding their targets. However, they discovered that only 60% of eligible workflows had adopted the solution. By addressing adoption barriers (training, change management, process updates), they increased adoption to 85% and realized an additional 15% in value. They documented the lessons learned and scaled the pattern to 3 other manufacturing sites, multiplying the value impact.
